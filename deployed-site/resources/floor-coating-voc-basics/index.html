VOC Basics for Floor Coatings

Get VOC basics for Edmonton floor coating decisions: what labels mean, how ventilation fits garage and commercial installs, and which proposal questions to ask.

Updated
September 8, 2026

Ask Denis about your floor

VOC means volatile organic compound. In a floor-coating decision, a VOC number can help compare a defined environmental property of products when the calculation basis and category match. It cannot by itself tell you whether the coating is odourless, non-toxic, safe for immediate occupancy, durable, or appropriate for a particular slab.

What “volatile” describes

Health Canada describes VOCs as a large group of chemicals present in indoor and outdoor air and identifies products such as paints and solvents among indoor sources (Health Canada VOC guidance). “Volatile” concerns a substance’s tendency to enter the air; it is not a complete hazard ranking.

Different VOCs have different health effects, odour thresholds, and exposure limits. A floor system may also contain important non-VOC hazards, including ingredients that affect skin or eyes. That is why VOC content, hazard communication, ventilation, and floor performance remain separate review tracks.

Decode the number before comparing it

A product may publish VOC content in grams per litre, but comparisons can still fail if one value is:

  • for a single component rather than the mixed product;
  • calculated with or without water and exempt compounds;
  • measured under a different test or regulatory definition;
  • reported for a tint base rather than the finished colour;
  • based on a different regional formulation;
  • for one layer while another proposal reports a complete system.

Ask the supplier for the current Canadian technical document and the exact basis of the number. Compare primer with primer, topcoat with topcoat, and whole system with whole system.

Environment and Climate Change Canada states that the federal Architectural Coatings Regulations establish VOC concentration limits for identified coating categories (ECCC regulatory overview). Regulatory compliance is a minimum legal question for an applicable category; it is not a claim that two compliant products have equal indoor-air, application, or service characteristics.

Five phrases that need a follow-up

Marketing phrase What it may communicate What to ask next
“Low VOC” VOC content lower than some reference Numeric value, basis, category, region, and layer
“Zero VOC” Value below a stated threshold or definition Definition, test basis, tint/additives, and full-system values
“100% solids” Manufacturer’s formulation/solids description SDS hazards, mixed-product handling, cure, and actual emissions data
“Water-based” Water is part of the carrier system Other ingredients, SDS controls, ventilation, and cure conditions
“Low odour” Sensory claim under unspecified conditions Product-specific evidence, site controls, and re-entry criteria

None of these phrases proves a product is harmless. None establishes wear resistance or adhesion either.

Use the SDS for hazards, not for sales comparison alone

CCOHS explains that SDSs contain sixteen sections addressing identification, hazards, composition, first aid, firefighting, accidental release, handling and storage, exposure controls and PPE, physical properties, stability, toxicology, and other information (CCOHS SDS guide).

For a two-component coating, obtain the SDS for each component and understand the mixed-product procedure. Include cleaners, repair products, primers, solvents, and topcoats. Review:

  • hazard classifications and statements;
  • routes of exposure;
  • required ventilation or engineering controls;
  • skin, eye, and respiratory protection;
  • incompatible materials and ignition concerns;
  • spill, first-aid, storage, and disposal instructions;
  • revision date and Canadian supplier information.

An SDS is a safety communication document. The product data sheet supplies application limits and performance information; the system specification connects the layers; the site hazard assessment determines controls.

VOC content is not an emissions timeline

Content describes material composition under a defined calculation. Indoor concentration over time depends on quantity, surface area, application and cure, temperature, air exchange, room volume, air pathways, and other products in use. A low content number does not automatically produce a safe re-entry time for every building.

Health Canada recommends source control and ventilation as ways to improve indoor air, including following label directions and increasing ventilation during renovations (Health Canada VOC guidance). For occupied work, translate that principle into a site plan: isolate the zone, identify exhaust and replacement-air routes, protect nearby intakes, manage open containers and waste, and define who authorizes access.

If a project requires an indoor-emissions certification or chamber-test result, state the exact required standard and product. Do not substitute a VOC-content value.

Questions for homes and occupied facilities

  • Which rooms and HVAC paths connect to the work area?
  • Can occupants relocate, and who may need a more conservative plan?
  • Where will exhaust discharge and replacement air enter?
  • Will winter heating or summer humidity affect ventilation and cure?
  • What happens to mixed leftovers, wipes, containers, and solvent?
  • Which product-specific criteria govern worker entry, other trades, and normal occupancy?
  • Who receives an odour or symptom report, and who can pause work?

Do not promise that a product will cause no symptoms. If someone has health concerns or experiences effects, move them away from exposure and seek appropriate medical or poison-control advice using the exact product information.

The decision rule

Use VOC data to compare like products under the same definition and to check applicable regulatory requirements. Use SDSs and a hazard assessment to manage health and safety. Use product data and system evidence to judge application and service. Use a ventilation and access plan to protect occupied spaces.

Keeping those four decisions separate makes the VOC number more useful, not less. It becomes one documented input instead of a vague promise about the whole floor.

This article is general education, not an exposure assessment or medical, regulatory, or ventilation determination. Use current Canadian documents and qualified site-specific advice.
