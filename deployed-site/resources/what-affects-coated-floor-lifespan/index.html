What Affects Coated Floor Life

Learn what affects coated floor lifespan in Edmonton: prep quality, diamond grinding, traffic, winter salt, cleaning habits, and system choice over slogans.

Updated
September 18, 2026

Ask Denis about your floor

There is no responsible universal answer to “How long will this floor coating last?” Service life depends on the concrete, preparation, complete layer sequence, application conditions, traffic, chemicals, sunlight, cleaning, impacts, water, and timely repairs. A garage used for storage and a forklift aisle do not create the same evidence.

For local scope options, see our commercial epoxy flooring.

Instead of shopping for one large number, build a record of conditions and ask how the proposed system addresses them.

The concrete sets the starting point

Coatings depend on a sound, suitable substrate. Weak concrete, contamination, old sealers, moving cracks, damaged joints, and unresolved water can affect the system above. Preparation must remove or treat the conditions identified in the written scope and create the surface required by current product documents.

The ACI PRC-546 concrete repair guide covers evaluation, preparation, repair, protective systems, and quality control. That framework supports treating substrate work as part of durability planning, not an invisible preliminary step. Ask what will be inspected before primer and how concealed deterioration changes the scope.

Moisture needs evidence

A slab that looks dry may still require a standardized moisture evaluation for the selected system. ASTM F2170 describes in-situ relative-humidity testing in concrete floor slabs. When specified, project records should include method, timing, locations, results, and the exact manufacturer acceptance criterion. A test supports selection; it does not create a blanket lifespan guarantee.

Exterior drainage, plumbing, joints, wash practices, and seasonal meltwater also deserve review. A coating should not be used to hide an active water source.

System design should match a use map

List traffic by people, vehicles, casters, forklifts, carts, and concentrated stationary loads. Mark turning, braking, impact, welding, chemical handling, wash areas, direct sun, loading doors, and Edmonton winter grit or de-icer. Give the map to the specifier and ask which layer addresses each exposure and which requires another control.

A topcoat, primer, broadcast, mat, tray, guard, drain, or traffic-rule change may each have a role. No single layer is automatically the durability layer. Read the guide to what a topcoat adds before comparing proposals by coat count alone.

Application records matter later

Current manufacturer instructions govern mixing, application, environmental limits, recoat timing, and cure. Ask who records substrate and air conditions, material batches, mix times, coverage areas, and inspection results. Records make a later problem easier to diagnose and show whether the planned process was followed.

Edmonton doorways and perimeter zones may be cooler or wetter than the centre of a heated building. Measurements should represent the actual work area where required. If conditions fall outside limits, the schedule should allow work to pause.

Maintenance changes the wear curve

Loose grit under tires, shoes, pads, and wheels can abrade a finish. Residue from an unsuitable cleaner can change appearance or traction. Delayed spill removal can extend chemical contact. Obtain written maintenance instructions at handover and train the people who will use them.

Record cleaner, dilution, pad or brush, equipment, and frequency. Use entrance controls appropriate to the site, inspect furniture protection and wheels, contain leaks, and lift rather than drag movable loads. The scratch-maintenance guide offers a response ladder that starts with identification instead of aggressive cleaning.

Small damage deserves early attention

Photograph chips, cuts, lifting edges, worn texture, cracks, dampness, and unusual colour change. A local repair may keep water or contaminants out of exposed layers, but only if preparation and compatibility are established. Repeated damage in the same lane should trigger a broader review of traffic, load, housekeeping, and system selection.

Do not assume every cosmetic change is failure. Gloss reduction, transfer marks, colour variation, wear, and debonding are different conditions. Diagnose before repairing.

Ask for conditions, not a headline

A meaningful service-life or warranty discussion identifies the exact system, substrate assumptions, use, exclusions, maintenance, inspection obligations, and remedy. Ask:

  1. What observed site conditions support this system?
  2. Which traffic and chemical exposures were considered?
  3. What preparation and acceptance checks are documented?
  4. What cure stages apply to each kind of use?
  5. What routine care and inspection are required?
  6. Which damage should be reported immediately?
  7. What changes in use require reassessment?
  8. Which statements come from current manufacturer documents?

Keep the proposal, product and safety sheets, batch information, photos, test reports, maintenance instructions, and repair history. That file is more useful than a category claim years later.

No article can predict a duration for an unnamed floor in unknown service. Denis must verify any company-specific product, service, schedule, warranty, price, credential, or performance statement before it appears on a quote. The practical goal is to control the factors that can be observed and documented, then reassess when use changes.

Review the floor as use changes

Schedule condition reviews around the facility’s risk, not a generic calendar. Entrances may deserve attention after winter; warehouses after route or equipment changes; workshops after a new chemical or process; garages after a leak or vehicle change. Compare the same mapped locations and lighting where practical.

Look for changes in gloss, texture, colour, chips, lifting, cracks, joints, dampness, and cleaning effort. Record observations without labeling every change failure. Escalate soft areas, exposed concrete, spreading debonding, chemical damage, displaced cracks, or water promptly. A qualified assessment can then decide whether maintenance, a local repair, a broader recoat, or substrate investigation is appropriate.

Track total use changes rather than relying on age alone. New casters, heavier storage, sharper turns, hotter work, longer chemical contact, or more frequent washing can alter exposure even when the floor looks unchanged.
