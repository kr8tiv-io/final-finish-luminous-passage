Floor Coating Terminology Guide

Decode floor coating terminology like profile, diamond grind, flake broadcast, primer, topcoat, and cure so Edmonton buyers can read proposals without fog.

Updated
September 8, 2026

Ask Denis about your floor

Flooring terms often collapse a detailed requirement into one word. Use this glossary to expand the shorthand, then ask which product, test method, condition, or acceptance value applies to your project.

For local scope options, see our residential epoxy flooring.

Substrate and preparation

Abrasion: Mechanical wearing of a surface. In preparation, abrasion may remove a layer and create texture; the scope should name the method and required result.

Bond-inhibiting contaminant: Material such as oil, dust, curing compound, adhesive, wax, or incompatible sealer that can interfere with adhesion. Identification and removal must be method-specific.

Concrete surface profile (CSP): ICRI’s visual and tactile scale for comparing prepared-concrete roughness. ICRI 310.2R provides benchmark profiles and describes preparation methods and limitations (ICRI 310.2R). CSP does not by itself prove cleanliness, soundness, or moisture suitability.

Diamond grinding: Preparation using rotating diamond tooling. It may remove coatings, laitance, or high spots and leave a particular profile, depending on tools, concrete, and technique.

Laitance: A weak, fine surface layer on concrete. If present, it is removed to reach sound concrete rather than used as the bonding surface.

Mechanical preparation: Use of equipment such as grinders, shot blasters, or scarifiers to remove material and create the specified substrate condition. The equipment name is not an acceptance criterion.

Scarifying: Rotary cutting with flails or cutters that removes concrete and leaves an aggressive texture. It is distinct from grinding and may require later refinement.

Shot blasting: Propelling and recovering abrasive shot in a contained machine to clean and profile concrete. Edges and inaccessible areas need separate detailing.

Sound concrete: Concrete capable of supporting the intended repair or coating. “Looks solid” is not a defined test; the specification should state how soundness is assessed where it matters.

Cracks, joints, and repairs

Control or contraction joint: An intentional weakened plane intended to manage shrinkage cracking. The flooring detail should say whether it remains active, is filled, or is carried through.

Construction joint: Interface between separate concrete placements. It may require a different detail from a random crack.

Crack chasing: Cutting or opening a crack to a controlled geometry for a selected repair. It does not diagnose why the crack formed or whether it moves.

Delamination: Separation within concrete or between layers. A surface can remain in place while sounding hollow or lacking reliable support.

Feather edge: A repair tapering to nearly zero thickness. Many repair products prohibit it; use the manufacturer’s permitted edge detail.

Isolation or expansion joint: A joint designed to accommodate movement between elements. Rigidly bridging it can defeat the intended detail.

Repair mortar: Material used to replace removed or missing concrete. Select it by repair geometry, service, substrate, cure, and compatibility—not strength number alone.

Spall: Localized concrete loss, usually deeper than superficial scaling. The full weak boundary may extend beyond the visible hole.

Moisture and environment

Dew point: The temperature at which air reaches saturation and condensation can form. Coating data often require the substrate to remain a stated margin above it; use the product’s actual instruction.

In-situ relative humidity: Percent relative humidity measured within a concrete slab using probes under ASTM F2170. ASTM notes the result represents the slab condition at the time of testing (ASTM F2170-19a).

Moisture mitigation: A named system intended to manage slab moisture under defined conditions. It is not a universal cure for water entry or a substitute for testing.

Moisture vapour emission rate (MVER): A result from a specified emission test, not another name for internal relative humidity or a handheld meter reading.

Relative humidity (air): Amount of water vapour in air relative to the maximum possible at that temperature. Distinguish ambient RH from in-slab RH.

Vapour retarder: Material intended to limit vapour transmission within a building assembly. For an existing slab, its presence and condition may be unknown unless construction records establish them.

Products and system build

Binder: Film-forming portion that holds a coating together and bonds it within its intended system.

Broadcast: Aggregate, quartz, or flake distributed into a wet layer. “Full broadcast” still needs a product quantity, rejection point, and finished texture standard.

Body coat: Main resinous layer providing much of the system build or encapsulating broadcast material.

Dry-film thickness (DFT): Thickness after a coating has cured or dried. It differs from wet-film thickness and theoretical coverage.

Epoxy: A class of thermosetting resin systems commonly formed by mixing resin and hardener. The word alone says nothing certain about thickness, UV behaviour, cure, or suitability.

High-build: Relative product description indicating a thicker applied film than conventional thin coatings. Require a numeric specified thickness.

Primer: First system layer selected to interact with the prepared substrate and later coat. It does not replace preparation unless the system explicitly says so.

Resinous flooring system: Compatible sequence of repair, primer, resin, aggregate, body coat, grout coat, and/or topcoat. Not every project uses every layer.

Solids content: Portion remaining after volatile components leave, expressed according to the manufacturer’s stated basis. It is not a complete performance or safety rating.

Topcoat: Final resinous layer selected for surface properties such as finish, texture, cleanability, light stability, or exposure within a documented system.

Wet-film thickness (WFT): Thickness immediately after application. Some liquid coatings use WFT checks as installation quality control.

Application and cure

Cure: Chemical or physical progression by which material develops intended properties. Cure rate depends on product and conditions; dry-to-touch is not full cure.

Induction time: Manufacturer-specified wait after mixing before application for certain products. Never assume one exists or omit it; follow the data sheet.

Mix ratio: Required proportion of components by volume or weight. Container sizes do not authorize casual partial batching.

Open time: Period during which a layer or adhesive remains able to receive the intended operation under stated conditions. Manufacturers may use this term differently, so verify the definition.

Pot life: Time mixed material remains usable under specified conditions. Material in a pail may behave differently from material spread on a floor; follow product instructions.

Recoat window: Minimum-to-maximum interval for applying a subsequent layer under stated preparation and conditions. A missed maximum can require a documented recovery procedure.

Return to service: Point when a defined use is permitted. Foot traffic, vehicles, washing, chemical exposure, and full cure may have separate times.

Wet edge: Fresh boundary maintained so adjacent material blends during application. Crew size, batch planning, and room geometry influence it.

Documents and verification

Mock-up: Representative installed sample used to agree on appearance, texture, edge detail, or execution standard. A small sample on perfect concrete may not represent the floor.

Product data sheet (PDS/TDS): Manufacturer’s technical document covering uses, limits, preparation, mixing, application, and performance data for a product. Confirm revision and region.

Safety data sheet (SDS): Standardized hazard-communication document. CCOHS explains its sixteen sections, including hazards, first aid, handling, exposure controls, and disposal information (CCOHS SDS guide). It is not a durability specification.

Specification: Written requirements for products, execution, testing, acceptance, and closeout. A brand list without preparation and inspection is incomplete.

Submittal: Documents provided for review before work, such as product data, SDSs, colour samples, test reports, and proposed methods. Review does not erase contractor responsibilities or product limits.

Test area: Limited preparation or removal used to investigate the existing floor and establish a workable method. It differs from a decorative mock-up.

VOC: Volatile organic compound. A VOC concentration or content value addresses one environmental characteristic; it does not summarize all hazards, odour, or floor performance.

When a proposal uses one of these terms, add a margin note: “According to which document, measured how, and accepted by whom?” That simple habit converts vocabulary into project controls.

Definitions here are plain-language orientation, not substitutes for current standards, specifications, SDSs, or manufacturer documents.
