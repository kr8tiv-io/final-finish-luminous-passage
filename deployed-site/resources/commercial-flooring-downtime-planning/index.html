Commercial Floor Downtime Plan

Build a commercial floor coating downtime plan for Edmonton spaces covering phased access, cure timing, diamond-grind dust control, and return-to-service steps.

Updated
September 8, 2026

Ask Denis about your floor

“How many days will we be closed?” is the wrong first scheduling question. Start with the business events that cannot overlap the work: customers crossing the area, forklifts serving a dock, sanitation cycles, emergency egress, deliveries, food preparation, patient movement, or access to electrical and mechanical rooms. Then build the coating sequence between those constraints.

For local scope options, see our commercial epoxy flooring.

The result is a shutdown plan with decision gates, not a promise based on a calendar alone.

Create an operations map before requesting dates

Walk the area with the facility lead, installer, health-and-safety contact, and the person who can authorize reopening. Mark:

  • pedestrian, cart, pallet-jack, and vehicle routes;
  • exits, fire equipment, panels, drains, coolers, and shutoffs;
  • production equipment that stays, moves, or needs disconnection;
  • adjacent occupants and shared ventilation;
  • receiving hours, cleaning shifts, and contractor access;
  • temperature-sensitive inventory;
  • noise-, dust-, or odour-sensitive operations;
  • the smallest zones that can function independently.

Alberta’s official OHS page links the current Act, Regulation, and Code that govern Alberta workplaces (Alberta OHS legislation). The owner and contractors should decide who controls the work area, communicates hazards, manages simultaneous work, and verifies that required routes remain available.

Break “downtime” into five clocks

A proposal that lists only application time hides most schedule risk.

Clock Starts when Ends when Common dependency
Operational clear-out Inventory or equipment begins moving Area is released to contractor Labour, lockout, disconnection
Preparation Protection and surface work begin Substrate passes inspection Removal difficulty, repairs, dust control
Layer sequence First material is mixed Last specified layer is complete Recoat windows, temperature, humidity
Restricted cure Last layer is placed Defined traffic is permitted Product data and actual site conditions
Recommissioning Protection is removed Normal operations resume Cleaning, equipment setup, owner acceptance

These clocks can overlap only when the site plan says they can. For example, moving equipment in Zone B while grinding Zone A may be possible, but only if traffic, dust, noise, and emergency access remain controlled.

Product timelines are condition-dependent. As a bounded example, Sikafloor-217 lists different recoat, foot-traffic, light-traffic, and full-cure times at 10°C, 20°C, and 30°C (Sika product data). Those figures are not a schedule for another system; they show why the project plan must reference the exact product sheets and recorded conditions.

Use gates instead of optimistic hand-offs

Put an owner and required evidence beside every gate.

Gate 1 — area released: equipment disposition is complete; services are isolated where required; access and emergency arrangements are active.

Gate 2 — substrate accepted: preparation is complete; hidden contamination and damage have been assessed; repairs, moisture results, and surface condition are documented.

Gate 3 — ready for next layer: elapsed time and ambient/substrate conditions meet the current product instructions; contamination has not occurred; any maximum recoat requirement is still satisfied.

Gate 4 — restricted traffic: the named product criteria for that traffic class are met. “Dry to touch” is not automatically permission for carts, shelving, washing, or vehicle loads.

Gate 5 — operational reopening: barriers can be removed; required cleaning and inspection are complete; equipment and routes are safely restored; the owner accepts outstanding items.

If a gate fails, the plan needs a branch: repair and re-inspect, mechanically prepare an overdue layer as instructed, extend environmental control, reopen another zone, or move the launch date.

Design zones around complete work packages

Phasing reduces the area unavailable at one time but adds edges, transitions, mobilizations, and coordination. Do not divide the floor into arbitrary rectangles. Each zone should have:

  • a safe independent entry and exit;
  • enough room for preparation and dust collection;
  • a deliberate termination detail between phases;
  • a route for waste out and materials in;
  • protection from traffic in adjacent live zones;
  • a defined cleaning and acceptance point.

A checkerboard plan usually creates more boundary and traffic problems than it solves. Long strips, complete rooms, or operational cells are often easier to isolate, but the actual choice depends on equipment, joints, drainage, and workflow.

Account for the work around the coating

Commercial downtime is frequently consumed by tasks omitted from the coating quote: disconnecting racks, removing old flooring, testing unknown residues, curing repair materials, relocating inventory, resetting equipment, and cleaning construction dust.

CCOHS recommends keeping aisles and exits clear, providing orderly storage, and cleaning spills promptly as part of workplace housekeeping (CCOHS housekeeping guide). Translate that into the shutdown plan: designate material staging, waste routes, cord and hose management, spill response, and who maintains adjacent live areas.

Also list the facilities that cannot be isolated—washrooms, coolers, server rooms, loading docks—and determine whether coating work can proceed safely around them.

Build the run sheet backwards

Begin with the required reopening event and work backwards:

  1. normal operations restored;
  2. equipment and inventory returned;
  3. owner closeout and cleaning complete;
  4. permitted traffic threshold reached;
  5. final coat placed under acceptable conditions;
  6. preceding recoat gates met;
  7. repairs and prepared substrate accepted;
  8. area cleared and controlled.

Add contingency at the steps with real uncertainty, especially removal, repairs, moisture findings, temperatures, and maximum recoat windows. Name one decision-maker who can trade scope, phase, or reopening date if an assumption fails.

The final schedule should include product-sheet references, shift times, condition logs, inspection gates, communication contacts, and alternative routes. That document is more useful than a single “three-day system” label because it connects the material sequence to the way the facility actually operates.

This is planning guidance, not a safe-work procedure. The employer, prime contractor, and qualified project team must apply current Alberta requirements, product safety data, and site-specific controls.
