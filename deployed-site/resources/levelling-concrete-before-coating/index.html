Levelling a Floor Before Coating

Levelling concrete before a floor coating in Edmonton: flat versus level, grinding high spots, filling low spots, underlayments, and why garage floors slope.

Updated
September 22, 2026

Ask Denis about your floor

Why levelling comes before coating

A floor coating is thin. It follows the concrete under it, so a dip, a lip, or a ridge in the slab shows through the finished floor. Levelling deals with those first: grinding down what is high, filling what is low, and smoothing the transitions. Final Finish does concrete levelling and restoration as part of its concrete work, alongside repair and diamond grinding.

Flat is not the same as level

Two words, two different things:

  • Flat means the surface has no bumps or dips over a short distance. A flat floor can still slope.
  • Level means the floor is horizontal overall.

Garages are usually not meant to be level. They slope toward the overhead door or a floor drain so meltwater runs out instead of pooling. Levelling a garage does not mean taking that slope away. It means taking out the low spots where water sits, the lips at cracks, and the ridges at old patches, so the floor drains the way it was built to.

Large commercial floors put numbers on this. ASTM E1155 defines the FF and FL numbers used to measure flatness and levelness. Most homes do not need that precision, but the idea is the same: measure, then correct.

Finding the problems

  • A long straightedge, laid across the floor in different directions, shows dips and humps. Light under the edge means a low spot.
  • Water tells the truth in a garage. After washing the floor, watch where the puddles stay.
  • A laser level maps a larger floor quickly.

Note the lips at cracks, where one side has lifted, and the edges of old patches. Those catch tires and show through a coating as ridges.

Grinding the high spots

Diamond grinding brings down high spots, lips, and ridges. The same grinding that prepares the surface for the coating can take a little more off where the floor stands proud. It is precise, and it is also finite: grinding removes millimetres, not centimetres. The diamond grinding guide explains the equipment and the edge work.

Filling the low spots

Low spots, pits, and spalls are filled with repair materials made for the job. The material has to suit the depth, the traffic, and the coating that goes over it. The ICRI guide to selecting repair materials is the reference for matching a material to a repair.

For larger areas, a self-levelling underlayment or topping can bring a section back to plane. It has to be compatible with the coating system and allowed to cure before coating. Our repair mortars guide covers the options.

The door strip, the step, and other transitions

Transitions are where most levelling work happens in a house:

  • The overhead door strip. The concrete under the door often has a lip, a dip where water sits, or a broken edge from years of freeze-thaw. It is ground, filled, and finished so the door seals and water runs out.
  • The step into the house. The edge of the step chips and the floor at its base collects water. Both are repaired before coating.
  • Where a basement floor meets another floor. A coated slab next to carpet or hardwood needs a transition strip, and the heights should be planned before the coating goes down.

Old tile, glue, and patches

Basements and older garages often carry the history of past floors. Tile adhesive leaves ridges, old patches leave edges, and some past repairs were never feathered into the slab. Grinding removes adhesive and brings patch edges down flush. Where a patch is loose or hollow, it comes out and is redone with a compatible material, because a coating over a failing patch fails with it.

Test the drainage before coating

After grinding and filling, a garage floor should be tested the simple way: wet it and watch where the water goes. Any spot that still holds water is easier to fix now than after the coating is down. On a floor with a drain, the water should run to the drain; on one without, it should run out the door.

When levelling is not the answer

Some unevenness is a symptom. A slab that has settled, heaved, or cracked through may still be moving, and a patch or a coating will not stop that. The ACI guide to floor and slab construction explains how slabs shrink and curl.

If a crack is widening, a section rocks underfoot, or the floor has dropped along a wall, the cause needs looking at first. Sometimes the honest answer is that a coating is not the answer yet. The cracks and spalling guide helps sort cosmetic damage from structural questions.

What to expect in a quote

Levelling work should be named in the quote: where the floor will be ground down, where it will be filled and with what, and whether the slope to the door or drain is kept. Questions people ask

Should a garage floor be level?
Usually not. Garage floors are built to slope toward the door or a drain so meltwater runs off. Levelling a garage means removing dips, lips, and ridges while keeping that slope.
Can a floor coating hide an uneven floor?
No. A coating is thin and follows the concrete under it, so dips, lips, and ridges show through. They are ground down or filled before the coating goes on.
What is the difference between a flat floor and a level floor?
A flat floor has no bumps or dips over a short distance. A level floor is horizontal overall. A floor can be flat and still sloped, which is exactly what most garages are.
Can every uneven floor be fixed before coating?
Not always. Settlement, heave, or cracks that are still moving point to a problem below the surface. The cause needs looking at before any levelling or coating.
