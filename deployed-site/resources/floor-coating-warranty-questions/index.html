Floor Coating Warranty Questions

Ask clear floor coating warranty questions in Edmonton about coverage scope, prep exclusions, garage tire exposure, and what documents to keep after install.

Updated
September 18, 2026

Ask Denis about your floor

A warranty is a map of responsibility

A floor-coating warranty can be useful, but the headline term is only the cover page. The practical value is in the definitions: what is covered, who decides whether a failure qualifies, what evidence is required, and what the owner must do after installation. Read the warranty together with the proposal, product data sheets, maintenance instructions, and the documented condition of the concrete.

This matters in Edmonton because a garage or commercial floor may encounter tracked grit, de-icing residue, wet vehicles, temperature swings at doorways, and pre-existing cracks. A warranty should not be treated as proof that those conditions have been assessed. It should explain how responsibility is handled if a problem appears.

First, identify who promises what

Ask the contractor to separate three things in writing:

  1. Installer workmanship. This may address whether the specified preparation and application were completed correctly.
  2. Manufacturer product coverage. This is governed by the manufacturer’s current written terms, not the contractor’s summary of them.
  3. Existing concrete and building conditions. Slab movement, moisture, contamination, drainage, and earlier repairs may sit outside either promise unless the contract says otherwise.

The distinction is important when a system contains materials from more than one manufacturer. Ask who owns compatibility between the primer, body coat, decorative media, repair products, and topcoat. A list of brand names is not the same as a system specification.

Questions that reveal the real scope

Bring the warranty document to the site assessment and work through these points.

What counts as a covered defect?

Does the document define peeling, delamination, blistering, discolouration, cracking, loss of gloss, or wear? Are cosmetic changes treated differently from loss of bond? General language such as “coating failure” leaves room for disagreement.

Which areas are included?

Confirm edges, vertical curbs, steps, control joints, repaired cracks, drains, door thresholds, and areas beneath fixed equipment. If one of these receives different treatment, the proposal and warranty should say so.

What substrate assumptions apply?

Concrete is part of the system. The ACI concrete-repair guide covers condition evaluation, surface preparation, repair, and quality control; that breadth is a useful reminder that a coating promise cannot be evaluated separately from its substrate. Ask what cracks, spalls, contamination, previous coatings, or moisture indicators were found and how each is reflected in the scope.

If moisture is relevant, ask what test method, locations, dates, results, and product limits will be documented. ASTM F2170 defines an in-situ relative-humidity test method for concrete slabs. Naming that standard is not enough: the proposal still needs to state whether it will be used and how results will be compared with the selected manufacturer’s limits. For more context, read the guide to concrete moisture testing before coatings.

Which uses or exposures are excluded?

Ask specifically about tires, welding, dragging steel, snow-removal tools, battery acid, solvents, standing water, de-icing chemicals, UV exposure, and unusually hot equipment. The correct answer may be an exclusion, a different system, or an operating precaution. What matters is deciding before installation.

What maintenance keeps coverage valid?

Request the approved cleaner type, dilution method, cleaning frequency, tools, spill-response steps, and any required inspections or recoats. Avoid accepting “clean as needed” as the entire maintenance plan. The owner should be able to follow the instructions without guessing. Our coated garage floor cleaning guide shows the level of practical detail worth looking for.

Ask how a claim actually works

A usable warranty should answer operational questions, not just material questions:

  • Who receives notice, and by what method?
  • Is there a deadline after the issue is found?
  • What photographs, invoices, maintenance records, or access are required?
  • Who inspects the floor and who makes the coverage decision?
  • Does the remedy mean spot repair, recoating an area, supplying material, or something else?
  • Who pays for moving equipment, closing the space, surface preparation, and disposal?
  • If a repair differs visually from the surrounding floor, is that considered acceptable?

Keep the final signed proposal, warranty, batch or product records supplied by the installer, photographs, care instructions, and correspondence in one project file. Take dated overview and close-up photographs before the space returns to use. Documentation helps both sides distinguish a localized incident from a broader system concern.

Watch for language that needs clarification

Pause when a warranty relies on undefined phrases such as “lifetime,” “normal use,” “proper maintenance,” or “subject to inspection.” These phrases may be legitimate, but they need definitions. Also ask whether the term transfers when a property is sold, whether commercial and residential uses receive different terms, and whether repairs restart or merely continue the original period.

Do not infer coverage from a verbal assurance, sample board, or product brochure. A sample demonstrates appearance. A data sheet describes a product. Neither automatically establishes the contractor’s remedy if the installed floor develops a problem.

Compare warranties only after comparing scopes

A longer stated term does not necessarily provide broader protection. One proposal may include detailed substrate testing, repairs, edge work, and maintenance documentation; another may offer a longer headline with narrower exclusions. Normalize the actual work first using the floor-coating proposal comparison guide, then compare the warranty attached to that work.

Before signing, ask the contractor to mark every company-specific promise directly in the current document.
