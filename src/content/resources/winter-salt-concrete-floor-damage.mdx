Edmonton winters bring meltwater, grit, and de-icing chemicals onto garage, parkade, loading, and entrance floors. The visible white residue is only part of the story. Water movement, repeated wetting, freezing exposure at edges, existing cracks, and concrete quality all affect what you see. A coating is not a substitute for diagnosing and repairing deteriorated concrete.

## Read the official Alberta guidance first

Alberta Infrastructure’s technical bulletin on [de-icing concrete](https://www.alberta.ca/system/files/custom_downloaded_images/tr-tb-001a.pdf) discusses de-icer effects on concrete and calls for thorough spring washing of treated concrete. Use that primary source to frame seasonal care, while following site requirements for containment and discharge. Do not send salty or contaminated wash water to a drain without confirming it is permitted.

The bulletin is not a project-specific repair specification. Interior heated slabs, exposed aprons, ramps, and thresholds experience different water and temperature conditions.

## Make a winter condition map

Before deep cleaning, photograph tire paths, door lines, drains, joints, wall edges, ramps, and wet spots. Mark:

- loose or flaking surface material;
- shallow scaling or deeper spalls;
- cracks and displaced edges;
- rust staining or exposed metal;
- white deposits and persistent dampness;
- hollow or failed patches;
- damage at the warm-to-cold transition;
- routes where vehicles or footwear deposit slurry.

Repeat photographs after cleaning and drying. Appearance alone cannot establish depth or cause, but the pattern helps an assessor distinguish deposits from material loss and choose investigation points.

## Control water and tracked material

Repair leaks and review drainage, door seals, downspouts, grading, and snow-storage practices with appropriate trades. Remove standing water promptly using a method suitable for the facility. Entrance mats and containment can reduce tracking but need cleaning and drying so they do not become saturated reservoirs.

Sweep or vacuum grit before wet cleaning to reduce abrasion. Use cleaners and equipment permitted for the concrete or installed coating. Never mix cleaning products. Follow labels and safety data sheets, protect occupants, and manage runoff.

## Damaged concrete needs a repair decision

Soundness, contamination, cracks, reinforcing corrosion, joints, and moisture must be evaluated before coating. The [ACI PRC-546 concrete repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) covers evaluation, concrete removal, preparation, repair materials, protective systems, and quality control. That primary framework supports mapping deterioration and selecting repairs before a protective finish.

Deep spalling, exposed or corroded reinforcement, significant displacement, structural concern, or continuing water entry may require an engineer or other qualified professional. Do not fill a moving joint or unstable spall simply to create a flat decorative surface.

The [concrete cracks and spalling guide](/resources/concrete-cracks-spalling-floor-coating/) helps organize observations for an assessment. Ask how repair edges are cut or prepared, how unsound material is identified, which repair product is compatible, and what cure occurs before coating.

## Coating selection starts after evaluation

Request the complete layer sequence and current manufacturer requirements for substrate, preparation, moisture, temperature, application, cure, chemical exposure, and cleaning. Ask which winter exposures the recommendation is intended to address and which remain owner controls.

Avoid blanket claims that a coating is “salt proof” or will stop freeze-thaw damage. The result depends on the exact system, concrete, detailing, exposure, defects, and maintenance. A coating also cannot correct drainage or stop water entering from an unresolved source.

At door thresholds and ramps, define terminations deliberately. Snowplows, shovels, tire chains, metal edges, and abrupt transitions may create mechanical damage that needs operational controls or sacrificial details.

## Plan seasonal maintenance at handover

The owner should receive approved cleaning products, dilution, tools, frequency, spill response, inspection points, and prohibited methods. Include a spring inspection after accumulated winter material is removed. Look for chips, worn texture, lifting edges, cracks, dampness, and damaged terminations.

Document changes early. A small local repair may be possible when the boundary is sound and compatibility is known; repeated or widespread damage needs a broader diagnosis. Keep the winter log with product and repair records.

## Bring these questions to the quotation

1. Which observed areas show deposits, scaling, spalling, moisture, or previous repair?
2. What additional assessment is needed before a coating scope?
3. How will de-icer, oil, old coatings, and weak concrete be handled?
4. Which cracks, joints, drains, ramps, and thresholds are included?
5. What inspection proves the substrate is ready for the specified system?
6. What winter cleaning and runoff plan is compatible with the finish?
7. Which damage requires prompt review after installation?

Winter salt is a recurring exposure to manage, not a single stain to cover. ## Distinguish the heated floor from the exposed edge

An interior garage bay, overhead-door line, exterior apron, ramp, and wall perimeter may not share the same wetting or freezing exposure. Ask the assessor to divide them on the plan and explain each termination. A system selected for a conditioned interior should not be assumed suitable beyond the door.

Coordinate snow removal so blades, chains, and stored piles do not damage terminations or hold salty water against walls. Keep drains and collection points functional through the season. When spring cleaning reveals aggregate loss, hollow patches, widening cracks, or rust staining, pause cosmetic repair and obtain the appropriate concrete evaluation.

Owners should also record which de-icing products are used on site or tracked in by vehicles when known. Product names and safety sheets give the assessor more useful information than calling every residue “salt.”
