## How long should new concrete wait before it is coated?

Long enough to cure, and to dry to the coating's limit. New concrete gains strength over weeks, and it holds a lot of water while it does. Coating manufacturers set a minimum age and a moisture limit for their products, and a moisture test confirms the slab has reached it. On a new-build garage, that often means the coating goes down well after move-in.

It is worth the wait. A coating applied over a slab that is still wet can trap that moisture, then blister or lose its bond.

## Curing and drying are not the same thing

Curing is the chemical reaction that hardens concrete. It needs water and time, and it carries on for weeks after the pour. The [ACI guide to curing](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=30816) covers how curing is done and why it matters for strength and surface quality.

Drying is the slab giving up the extra water that was needed to place and finish it. A slab can be well cured and still too wet for a coating. That is why a moisture test is the real gate, not the calendar. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) describes measuring relative humidity inside the slab, and the product documents say what reading is acceptable. Our [moisture testing guide](/resources/concrete-moisture-testing-floor-coatings/) explains the options.

## What the builder may have put on the surface

New slabs often get a curing compound or a sealer sprayed on after finishing. It helps the concrete cure. It also stops a coating from bonding, so it has to come off, and it is not always visible. Ask your builder what was applied, and keep the answer for your coating contractor.

New floors can also carry a weak, powdery top layer called laitance, left when water and fine material rise to the surface during finishing. It can look smooth and solid, but a coating bonded to it is only as strong as that thin layer. Grinding removes it. The [ICRI guideline on surface preparation](https://store.icri.org/item/3102r2013-english-selecting-concrete-surface-preparation-sealers-coatings-polymer-overlays-concrete-repair-342520) explains why the prepared surface has to suit the coating system, and new concrete is no exception.

## The finish on the slab matters too

Garage slabs are often trowelled to a smooth, dense finish. That surface is easy to sweep and hard for a coating to grip until it is ground open. A broom finish is rougher, but it can still carry laitance. Either way, the preparation is diamond grinding, and the [surface profile guide](/resources/concrete-surface-profile-csp/) explains what an open surface means in practice. The [diamond grinding guide](/resources/diamond-grinding-concrete-preparation/) covers the equipment.

## Cracks and joints in a new slab

New concrete shrinks as it dries. That is why slabs have control joints, cuts that give the shrinkage a place to crack neatly. Hairline cracks in the first months are common and usually cosmetic. The [ACI guide to floor and slab construction](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=302115) explains shrinkage, curling, and joint spacing.

For a coating, the useful questions are simple. Are the cracks stable, or still moving? Where are the joints, and will the coating bridge them or stop at them? Cosmetic crack filling is part of the preparation. A crack that keeps growing is a question for the builder first.

## Why the wait pays off

Waiting feels like lost time when the garage is right there. The alternative costs more. A coating over wet concrete can blister as moisture pushes up under it, or let go in patches where it never bonded properly. Fixing that means grinding the failed coating off and starting again, on a floor that now has to be prepared twice. A few extra weeks before the first coat is the cheaper path.

## Living with the slab before it is coated

If you move in before the floor is coated, a few habits keep the concrete ready for it:

- **Catch drips.** Oil and fluids soak into new concrete fast. A drip tray under each vehicle saves grinding later. The [oil stain guide](/resources/remove-oil-stains-before-floor-coating/) explains why soaked-in oil is hard to remove.
- **Rinse out the salt.** In the first winter, sweep and rinse meltwater and road salt out of the garage rather than letting it dry on the floor.
- **Skip the sealer.** If you plan to coat, ask the builder not to add a sealer or hardener on top, or at least to tell you exactly what went on.
- **Avoid rubber mats and tape.** Some leave residue or stains that have to be ground away.

## A new-build checklist

Before booking a coating on new concrete, gather:

- The pour date.
- What curing compound, sealer, or hardener was applied, if any.
- Whether the slab has in-floor heat. See [coating a heated garage floor](/resources/heated-garage-floor-coating/).
- Any cracks, and whether they have changed.
- Whether a moisture test has been done, when, and the result.
