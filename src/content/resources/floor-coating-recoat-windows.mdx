A recoat window is the period in which one layer may receive the next layer under the manufacturer’s stated conditions and preparation. It has two edges: too early and too late. “Apply tomorrow” is not a recoat specification because temperature, product, layer thickness, humidity, and the condition of the first coat can change the permitted timing.

## Minimum and maximum solve different problems

The **minimum** wait helps ensure the first layer has progressed far enough to accept the next operation without unacceptable disturbance, solvent entrapment, marking, or other product-specific problems.

The **maximum** wait marks the point beyond which the manufacturer may require additional cleaning, abrasion, primer, or another procedure to establish intercoat bond. The exact reason and remedy depend on the chemistry and system.

Do not confuse these times:

- pot life: usable time after components are mixed;
- working time: practical application period;
- tack-free or dry-to-touch time;
- recoat window;
- foot-traffic time;
- vehicle or equipment traffic time;
- full cure or chemical-service time.

They may appear on the same data sheet but govern different decisions.

## Why a universal number is impossible

As one bounded example, Sikafloor-217 publishes different minimum and maximum recoat times at 10°C, 20°C, and 30°C, alongside separate pot-life and traffic times ([Sikafloor-217 data](https://usa.sika.com/en/construction-products/flooring/flooring-products/epoxy-coatings/sikafloor-217.html)). A second Sika product, Sikafloor-7500, publishes a different set of waiting periods and also lists ambient, substrate, humidity, and condensation conditions ([Sikafloor-7500 data](https://usa.sika.com/en/construction-products/coatings-water-repellents/waterproofing/epoxy/sikafloor-7500.html)).

These examples are not recommendations for an Edmonton project. They demonstrate that even one manufacturer’s epoxy products do not share a single “epoxy recoat time.” Use the Canadian documents and written system instructions for the products actually supplied.

## Build a layer-by-layer timing matrix

Do this before mobilization, not after the first coat is down.

| From → to | Product documents | Site conditions used | Earliest start | Latest start | If missed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Repair → primer | Repair and primer PDS | Slab/air/material values | Calculated time | Calculated time | Written repair/primer procedure |
| Primer → body coat | Both layer documents | Recorded conditions | Calculated time | Calculated time | Clean/abrade/reprime as directed |
| Body coat → grout/topcoat | System and layer documents | Recorded conditions | Calculated time | Calculated time | Manufacturer-approved recovery |
| Topcoat → traffic | Topcoat document | Recorded cure conditions | By traffic class | Not applicable | Extend restriction/reassess |

Use actual dates and clock times. If a layer begins at 3:40 p.m., a 24-hour maximum is not “sometime the next day.” Add shift changes, inspection, masking, broadcast removal, cleaning, and condition verification before the deadline.

## Conditions belong beside the clock

Record air temperature, slab temperature, material temperature where required, relative humidity, and condensation margin at the locations and frequency specified for the system. A cold edge beside an overhead door may cure differently from the centre. Averaging conditions across the room can hide the limiting area.

Also log:

- product, component, lot, and batch;
- mixing and placement start/finish;
- area or zone;
- film or broadcast checks required by the specification;
- observed contamination, blush, tack, or damage;
- inspection and authorization for the next layer.

The record makes a later decision traceable. Without it, “we were within the window” is only a recollection.

## A missed window is a technical decision

Stop and protect the floor. Do not automatically place the next coat, and do not invent a sanding routine. Gather the layer product, elapsed time, actual conditions, surface observations, and current documents. Then obtain the manufacturer’s written recovery procedure where the data sheet does not already give one.

A recovery may require washing, cleaning, mechanical abrasion to a defined result, dust removal, solvent wiping where specifically permitted, a new primer, or removal and replacement. The correct sequence depends on the product and the contamination or cure state.

After recovery, inspect the whole affected zone, including edges and areas that received the earlier coat at different times. Document the deviation, instruction received, work performed, and revised next-layer window.

## Protect the first coat while waiting

“Waiting” is an active site condition. Restrict foot traffic and other trades. Prevent dust, insects, water, condensation, oil, overspray, cleaning residue, and heater exhaust from reaching the layer. Plan how broadcast aggregate is collected without contaminating adjacent uncured work.

If the space is occupied or products present chemical hazards, maintain the ventilation and access controls required by the hazard assessment. CCOHS explains that SDSs contain handling, storage, exposure-control, accidental-release, and other safety information ([CCOHS SDS guide](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/chemicals/whmis_ghs/sds.html)). Cure timing does not cancel those requirements.

## Schedule backwards from the hardest boundary

The hardest boundary is often a maximum recoat time, a building shutdown, or the earliest permitted return to a particular traffic class. Work backwards to set batch times, inspection shifts, environmental control, and contingency.

For multi-zone projects, use a zone map and separate clock for each placement. Staggering may make labour manageable, but it multiplies deadlines. Assign one person to maintain the live matrix and authorize layer transitions.

A credible proposal states the exact products, provisional windows at planned conditions, monitoring method, overnight controls, and missed-window procedure. That is the difference between a schedule built from chemistry and a schedule built from hope.

*Product data governs. This guide does not establish any recoat or traffic time. Use current Canadian PDSs and SDSs, recorded site conditions, and written manufacturer direction.*
