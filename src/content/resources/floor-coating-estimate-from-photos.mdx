## Can you get a floor coating estimate from photos?

Yes. Final Finish gives free estimates from photos or an in-person visit, and for most garages and basements, good photos get most of the way to a real number. The photos have to show what drives the price: the size and shape of the floor, the condition of the concrete, the damage, and anything the coating has to work around.

Here is what to send, how to take it, and what no photo can tell anyone.

## Start with the whole floor

Clear what you can, even if only for the photos. A floor hidden under storage bins cannot be judged. Then take four wide shots:

- **Two corner shots.** Stand in one corner and photograph the floor to the far corner. Then do the opposite corner. Together they show the whole slab.
- **From the doorway.** For a garage, stand outside the open overhead door and shoot in. This shows the door strip, the parking tracks, and the slope toward the door or drain.
- **From the back wall.** Turn around and shoot toward the door.

Shoot in daylight with the lights on. Avoid a flash pointed straight at the floor; it flattens texture and hides stains.

## Then the close-ups

Close-ups show what the preparation and repairs will involve:

- **Cracks.** One wide shot to show where they run, then a close-up with a coin or a tape measure beside the widest point for scale.
- **Stains.** Oil under the engine bay, rust marks, and anything dark or shiny.
- **Edges and damage.** Chips at the door, broken edges, and spots where salt has scaled the surface.
- **Old finishes.** Paint, a sealer, or an old coating, especially where it is peeling. A close-up of a lifting edge says more than a paragraph.
- **Drains, steps, and posts.** Anything the coating has to stop at or wrap around.
- **Signs of water.** White powdery deposits, damp corners, or water marks along a wall.

## Add the numbers and the story

Photos show the condition. A few facts fill in the rest:

- Rough measurements, wall to wall.
- How the space is used: parking, a workshop, a home gym, storage, or living space.
- Whether the space is heated, and whether the slab has in-floor heat.
- Any history of water, and whether the slab is new.
- The finish you are leaning toward. The [finish library](/finishes) and the [floor visualizer](/floor-visualizer) help if you are not sure yet.
- When you would like the floor done.

A short video works too. Walk slowly, keep the floor in frame, and say what you know about it.

## What photos cannot show

Photos show the surface. They do not show what is inside the slab. Three things usually need someone on site:

- **Moisture.** Moisture in concrete is measured, not seen. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) describes testing relative humidity inside the slab. A dry-looking floor in a photo does not rule it out.
- **The bond of old coatings.** Paint or an old coating can look sound and still come up in sheets once grinding starts.
- **Hollow or weak concrete.** Delaminated areas sound different when tapped. A camera cannot hear them.

That is why an estimate from photos is a starting point for the scope, not a promise about every square metre. The surface preparation has to suit the coating system, as the [ICRI guideline on surface preparation](https://store.icri.org/item/3102r2013-english-selecting-concrete-surface-preparation-sealers-coatings-polymer-overlays-concrete-repair-342520) sets out, and some of that can only be confirmed on the floor.

## Photo mistakes that slow an estimate

Most back-and-forth comes from a handful of habits:

- **Only close-ups.** A tight shot of a crack says nothing about where it runs or how much floor there is. Always send the wide shots first.
- **No scale.** A crack can look like a hairline or a canyon depending on the zoom. Put a coin, a pen, or a tape measure beside it.
- **A covered floor.** Bins, mats, and a parked car hide exactly what matters. Move what you can, even to one side.
- **Night light.** Warm bulbs turn grey concrete yellow and hide stains. Daylight through the open door is best for a garage.
- **Blurry phones.** Tap the screen to focus on the floor, hold still, and take two of each shot.

## When a visit is the better start

Photos work well for most garages and basements. A visit is often the better first step when the floor is large or commercial, when there is a known water problem, when an old coating is peeling in patches, or when a new slab may have been sealed and nobody knows with what. It also helps when the space is hard to photograph, such as a long shop with poor light. You can ask for either; the estimate is free both ways.

## What a good estimate tells you back

Whatever you send, the reply should be specific: the preparation the floor needs, which repairs are included, the system and finish proposed, what is not included, and what could change once the floor is ground. Alberta's [advice on hiring a contractor](https://www.alberta.ca/hiring-contractor) is to get estimates and changes in writing.
