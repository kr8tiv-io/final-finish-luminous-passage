Diamond grinding uses rotating abrasive segments to remove a controlled layer from concrete. In floor-coating work, its value is not that it makes concrete look scratched. It can remove certain coatings and surface contaminants, open the surface, flatten small projections, and expose defects that were hidden. The required result is a sound, clean, suitably profiled substrate—not a specified number of machine passes.

## What grinding can realistically do

Depending on the tooling and concrete, grinding may:

- remove paint, thin coatings, adhesive residue, laitance, or surface sealer;
- reduce ridges, trowel marks, and minor high spots;
- prepare edges and small rooms that larger equipment cannot access;
- blend patch boundaries;
- reveal cracks, weak areas, oil penetration, and previous repairs;
- produce a profile appropriate for some coating or repair materials.

ICRI 310.2R summarizes concrete preparation methods by capabilities, operating needs, limitations, and benchmark surface profiles ([ICRI 310.2R](https://store.icri.org/item/3102r2013-english-selecting-concrete-surface-preparation-sealers-coatings-polymer-overlays-concrete-repair-342520)). That source is useful precisely because method selection is not one-size-fits-all.

Grinding is not the same as repairing a low slab, diagnosing structural movement, removing deeply absorbed oil, or correcting water vapour. It also does not guarantee the texture required by every coating. The material requirement controls the target.

## The tooling has to match the layer

A project may need different segments for hard concrete, soft concrete, thick coatings, adhesive, or final refinement. Tool selection also changes scratch pattern, removal rate, heat, and the risk of smearing a contaminant instead of removing it.

Before full production, prepare a test area that includes the actual existing finish. The test should answer:

1. Does the tooling cut the existing layer cleanly?
2. What concrete profile remains?
3. Is the substrate sound beneath it?
4. Does residue load the tools or spread across the surface?
5. Can edge tooling produce a comparable result?
6. What defects become visible, and how will they be handled?

A machine and tooling log can help reproduce the work, but the floor itself remains the acceptance object.

## Dust control is part of grinding, not cleanup afterward

Grinding concrete can generate respirable crystalline silica. Alberta’s current “Silica at the work site” resource points employers to the health effects and legal requirements that apply where silica may be present ([Alberta OHS silica resource](https://ohs-pubstore.labour.alberta.ca/ch059)). Alberta’s OHS Code requires employers to minimize release of silica into the air as far as reasonably practicable and keep the work site clear of unnecessary accumulations ([Alberta OHS Code, Part 4](https://search-ohs-laws.alberta.ca/legislation/occupational-health-and-safety-code/part-4-chemical-hazards-biological-hazards-and-harmful-substances/)).

An appropriate work plan is developed by competent parties and may include source capture, suitable dust collectors and filters, equipment inspection, restricted access, housekeeping that avoids re-aerosolizing dust, exposure assessment, and required respiratory protection. A household vacuum and open exterior door are not a substitute for that plan.

Owners should ask where collected dust goes, how filters and bags are changed, how adjacent equipment is protected, and how the area is released for the next trade.

## Edges reveal whether the scope is complete

Large grinders cannot reach every wall, post, drain, stair, or equipment base. Edge grinders and hand tools behave differently, so an “edge halo”—a smoother or more heavily scratched strip—can remain.

The quote should identify:

- whether baseboards, shelving, or equipment block access;
- the width and finish standard for perimeter work;
- details at floor drains, control joints, columns, and thresholds;
- how vertical returns or coves are prepared;
- how edge dust is captured;
- whether inaccessible areas are excluded or require another method.

Do not wait until the colour coat to decide what “to the wall” meant.

## Inspect before the primer hides the evidence

Use a hold point after grinding and final cleaning. Walk the floor under strong side lighting and mark:

| Check | Acceptable evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Existing layer removal | No unapproved paint, adhesive, sealer, or soft residue remains |
| Profile | Representative areas match the product requirement and specified comparator |
| Soundness | Weak, hollow, scaling, or damaged areas are identified for repair |
| Cleanliness | Dust and loose abrasive material are removed; no oily smear is visible |
| Uniformity | Edges, centres, repairs, and tool transitions meet the same acceptance plan |
| Damage control | No unapproved gouges, exposed services, or excessive aggregate removal |
| Defect map | Newly visible cracks, spalls, and moisture observations are documented |

Profile alone does not prove cleanliness, moisture suitability, or bond strength. If the specification requires pull-off testing or another quantitative check, record the method, locations, and acceptance values separately.

## Grinding versus another method

Choose based on the substrate and finish, not equipment preference. Grinding may suit thin-layer removal, edge work, and projects needing controlled abrasion or some flattening. Shot blasting may offer efficient production and a different profile on open areas. Scarifying can remove more material but leaves a more aggressive texture. Multiple methods may be required across one floor.

Ask bidders to state what grinding will remove, the intended profile, dust controls, edge plan, repair assumptions, inspection method, and fallback if the test area fails. That turns “diamond grind included” from a vague line item into a verifiable preparation scope.

Keep the test-area photographs and preparation record in the closeout file. If a later coating problem is investigated, they show what was removed, what concrete was exposed, and where repairs were made before primer concealed the floor. The record is also useful when a future owner considers recoating or local repair.

*Concrete grinding presents serious dust, noise, electrical, and equipment hazards. This article is not a work procedure; qualified employers must meet current Alberta requirements and equipment instructions.*
