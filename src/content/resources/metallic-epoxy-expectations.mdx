## A practical inspection

## Expect a composition, not a photocopy

Metallic epoxy floors are valued for movement, depth, and variation created during placement. Those qualities also mean a small sample or inspiration photo cannot be reproduced line for line across a room. Approve the palette, contrast, overall character, and acceptable variation—not the precise location of every vein or highlight.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

Ask the installer to explain which aspects are controlled and which emerge from field application. Colour selection, product ratios, base colour, tools, and broad technique may be controlled. Pigment flow, overlap, temperature, working time, floor geometry, and lighting contribute to individual variation.

## Build a useful design brief

Replace adjectives such as “luxury” or “dramatic” with observable choices:

- dominant and secondary colours;
- low, medium, or strong contrast;
- calmer or more active movement;
- areas where a visual transition is acceptable;
- preferred sheen and texture;
- elements the floor should coordinate with;
- examples of effects you do **not** want.

View physical samples flat, under the room's actual lighting. Edmonton's long winter evenings make artificial light especially relevant. Look from doorways and seated height, not only directly above. Glossy finishes can reflect windows, fixtures, and surface shape strongly; test the room rather than judging a studio photograph.

## Ask what the sample approves

A labelled mock-up should identify primer or base, metallic product and colours, application method, and clear finish. Confirm whether it is meant to approve colour, texture, sheen, movement, or the whole package. A larger sample can communicate scale better but still cannot predict an exact room-sized pattern.

Keep the approved sample, photographs under neutral lighting, and exact product codes. Digital renderings are communication tools, not colour standards. Screens and cameras shift colour and contrast.

## The slab remains visible in subtle ways

A metallic effect may draw attention away from minor variation, but it does not level a slab, correct drainage, stop cracks, or strengthen unsound concrete. Directional reflections can make waves, patch edges, joints, and repairs more noticeable.

The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) links evaluation, repair, preparation, material selection, and quality control. Before design approval, map cracks, joints, previous patches, low areas, penetrations, and transitions. Ask which will remain visible and which create planned application boundaries.

Moisture questions also stay independent of appearance. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) defines one in-situ relative-humidity method; whether it is used and what result the metallic system accepts must be stated in product documentation and the project scope.

## Plan the whole-room application

Metallic placement benefits from a clear sequence and enough uninterrupted access. Discuss room geometry, columns, doorways, stairs, fixed objects, batch boundaries, crew roles, and the route out of the space. Ask where a stop can occur without creating an unintended visual line.

Dust, insects, water, and unplanned foot traffic can interrupt a decorative surface. Define barriers, ventilation, lighting, and who can enter while each layer develops. The current product documents should govern mixing, working time, environmental conditions, recoat, and return to use.

## Balance sheen, traction, and maintenance

The protective finish affects reflection, walking feel, and cleaning. No decorative name guarantees traction. Discuss wet use, footwear, slope, cleaning products, and any need for traction media. More texture may change both the look and cleaning effort.

Ask for a maintenance guide covering approved cleaners, tools, spill response, mats, furniture feet, and recoating. Use the [slip-resistance planning guide](/resources/slip-resistance-floor-coatings/) to describe real conditions rather than request a vague “non-slip metallic floor.”

## Repairs may remain visible

Metallic patterns are especially difficult to patch invisibly because the original flow cannot be recreated exactly and the surrounding surface changes with use. Before buying, ask how a localized scratch, chip, or substrate repair would be approached. A joint, doorway, or whole section may provide a more honest boundary than a tiny spot.

Retain leftover colour identifiers and the installed-system record, not unlabelled mixed product. A future professional needs to know every layer and its recoat preparation.

## Use three viewing moments at handover

Inspect the finished appearance after the manufacturer permits access, then again after temporary barriers and work lighting are removed. View it in daylight, normal evening light, and the primary direction of travel. Record agreed punch-list observations before furniture returns.

Judge the floor against the approved design brief, not a single social-media photograph. Natural variation that fits the agreed palette and movement is different from debris, an unintended stop line, missed edge, or coating defect. Ask the contractor to explain which observation is decorative variation and which requires correction under the written scope.

## Furnishing can change the composition

Large rugs, counters, display cases, and seating divide the visible pattern. Put a scaled furniture plan beside the floor plan before choosing a focal area. Confirm when rugs and pads may be placed and whether their backing is compatible. Furniture feet and moving methods should follow the coating and furniture suppliers' instructions.

If a future tenant may remove those objects, remember that covered and exposed areas can age differently. That possibility is another reason to keep the complete system and colour record rather than relying on a project photograph.

The written approval should name the complete system, palette, mock-up, acceptable variation, sheen or texture, planned transitions, preparation, repair assumptions, and maintenance.
