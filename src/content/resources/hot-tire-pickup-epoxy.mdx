## First confirm what actually happened

“Hot-tire pickup” is commonly used for damage or marking beneath tires, but the label is not a diagnosis. A dark tire imprint on top of a coating is different from softened film, a separated top layer, or a coating stack that has lifted to bare concrete. The repair depends on which condition exists.

Avoid aggressive scrubbing, sanding, or spot coating before documentation. Move the vehicle if it can be done without enlarging damage, then photograph the whole parking bay, all four tire positions, close details with scale, and an unaffected comparison area. Side lighting can reveal raised edges or loss of film.

## Create a simple incident record

Write down the date, vehicle and tire position, length of the drive before parking, weather, garage conditions, how long the vehicle remained, recent cleaning, and when the floor was installed or last recoated. Record whether the same tire position is affected repeatedly and whether another vehicle causes the same pattern.

Collect the proposal, warranty, product data sheets, maintenance instructions, application photos, batch information supplied at handover, and previous correspondence. Do not assume every layer is epoxy; many decorative systems combine different material categories.

## Read the damage from top to bottom

An assessor should determine where separation occurred:

- residue sitting on an intact topcoat;
- damage within the topcoat;
- separation between coating layers;
- separation between coating and primer;
- separation at the concrete surface;
- weak concrete attached to the underside of the removed film.

That boundary directs the investigation. If concrete remains attached, substrate strength may be relevant. If layers separated cleanly, recoat timing, surface condition, contamination, or compatibility may need review. If the surface only carries a mark, start with the manufacturer's approved cleaning procedure rather than a repair assumption.

The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) addresses condition assessment, surface preparation, repair, and quality control. It does not diagnose this specific floor, but it supports examining the complete substrate-and-system chain instead of blaming a product category from appearance alone.

## Possible lines of inquiry—not conclusions

A useful assessment may consider concrete condition, mechanical preparation, oil or sealer contamination, moisture, repairs, primer selection, layer compatibility, mixing, film thickness, recoat windows, ambient and slab conditions, cure protection, vehicle return timing, cleaning chemistry, and tire exposure.

Each hypothesis needs evidence. A statement that “the tires were hot” does not establish the failure mechanism. Neither does a statement that “epoxy always does this.” Request the applicable manufacturer documents and compare actual job records with their requirements.

Moisture deserves proportionate consideration when the pattern or slab history suggests it. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) defines one in-situ concrete relative-humidity method, but a new test cannot reconstruct every condition present at installation. It should be interpreted with slab history, product limits, and other observations.

## What to do while the issue is open

Keep the area clean and dry using only documented care methods. Avoid parking repeatedly on a loose edge, dragging tools over it, or applying tape that could pull more film. Mark the boundary lightly outside the damage or use dated photographs to see whether it changes.

Contact the installer promptly through the process described in the warranty. Provide the incident record rather than only a close-up image. Ask whether they want the area left untouched for inspection and whether continued vehicle use could affect the assessment.

## Evaluate a proposed repair

A repair scope should state how the affected boundary will be found, what is removed, how concrete and remaining coating are prepared, which layers are replaced, how compatibility is established, and what visual difference may remain. It should also address why the cause is considered localized and what evidence supports returning the same system to that area.

Ask about colour, flake, and gloss variation. A technically sound localized repair may remain visible because the surrounding floor has aged and decorative broadcast is random. Natural boundaries such as joints may offer a clearer stopping point.

## Questions for the inspection meeting

Ask the assessor to show you the failure plane and explain what observation supports it. Then ask:

- Is the condition residue, cohesive damage, or loss of bond?
- Is concrete attached to the removed coating?
- Does the pattern align only with tire contact or extend beyond it?
- Which installation and maintenance records were reviewed?
- Which alternative causes were considered and ruled out?
- Does the proposed repair address a cause or only restore appearance?

Request a short written finding even if the conclusion remains uncertain. “Insufficient evidence” can be an honest result and may point to monitoring or broader sampling. Avoid allowing a tiny destructive test without first agreeing how the test area will be restored.

If parking positions differ, label samples and photographs carefully; mixing locations can erase the pattern an assessor needs. Note unaffected tires too, because absence at a comparable position helps frame the investigation. Finally, obtain separate restrictions for walking, vehicle return, cleaning, and chemical exposure.
