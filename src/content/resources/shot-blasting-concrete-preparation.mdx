Shot blasting is a mechanical preparation method. A machine propels steel media at concrete, then recovers media and debris through an integrated process. On the right open floor, it can produce a consistent prepared path. It is not a universal answer for oil contamination, weak concrete, edges, stairs, tight rooms, every old coating, or every specified surface profile.

For local scope options, see our [concrete preparation](/services/concrete-solutions).

The useful question is not “Do you shot blast?” It is “Why is this method appropriate for these areas and this coating system?”

## Start with the condition, not the machine

Before equipment arrives, map the slab. Record old coatings, adhesive, curing compounds if known, patches, cracks, joints, spalls, drains, trenches, embedded metal, low spots, and possible contamination. Mark fixed equipment and areas where the machine cannot travel. In an Edmonton facility, include wet loading-door zones and places that collect de-icer or oil.

Cleaning and contamination removal generally need resolution before blasting. Steel media is not a degreaser. Unknown coatings or mastics may require identification and a separate removal plan, especially where disturbance could create a health or disposal concern. Do not assume that every removed material can enter ordinary waste.

The [ACI PRC-546 concrete repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) addresses condition evaluation, preparation, repair, protective systems, and quality control. Its scope supports connecting the preparation method to the observed concrete and intended repair or protection. The specified coating manufacturer’s current documents still govern required cleanliness, profile, repairs, and environmental limits.

## What the process can—and cannot—cover

A self-propelled machine needs room and a workable path. Edges, corners, wall lines, beneath fixed equipment, around drains, and narrow transitions usually need another compatible method. Ask the bidder to draw those zones and state how the different preparation patterns will be blended. “Shot blast floor” is incomplete if twenty percent of the actual area is inaccessible.

Machine travel, media size and condition, concrete hardness, passes, old material, and operator decisions all affect the resulting surface. Avoid selecting by an assumed machine setting alone. The acceptance point should be the prepared surface required by the written system, inspected after dust and debris are removed.

Shot blasting can reveal conditions hidden by an old finish: soft or unsound concrete, deep cracks, weak patches, contamination, aggregate, and uneven previous preparation. The proposal needs a pause-and-document process for those findings rather than an automatic application over them.

## Profile must match the complete system

Surface profile is the texture created by preparation. Too little preparation may leave a poor bonding surface; too much texture can require additional material or leave peaks that affect coverage and finish. The target should come from the selected system’s current data and project specification.

Review [how concrete surface profile is specified](/resources/concrete-surface-profile-csp/) before comparing estimates. A profile comparator or other specified method can help document the result, but a photograph alone does not capture every aspect of cleanliness, soundness, or profile. Agree on inspection responsibility and acceptance criteria before production work.

## Dust control still requires planning

An integrated recovery system does not mean zero dust or zero exposure. Connections, filters, maintenance, emptying, adjacent grinding, cleanup, and waste handling matter. Isolate occupied areas, protect air intakes and sensitive equipment as the site plan requires, and prevent workers or the public from crossing the work zone.

For an Alberta workplace, the employer and contractor should consult current requirements through the [Alberta OHS Act, Regulation and Code](https://www.alberta.ca/ohs-act-regulation-code). The project’s hazard assessment, exposure controls, equipment instructions, and applicable legislation remain controlling.

Workers need task-specific procedures for machine operation, electrical supply, noise, airborne material, housekeeping, and waste. Those belong to the competent parties managing the work; a homeowner-facing article cannot prescribe them.

## Moisture testing is a different line item

Mechanical preparation does not establish moisture suitability. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) describes in-situ relative-humidity testing in concrete slabs. If the coating manufacturer or project specification calls for that method, the bid should identify timing, locations, conditioning, reporting, and product-specific acceptance criteria. Blasting and observing dry concrete is not an equivalent test.

## A proposal should answer these questions

1. What existing materials and contaminants were observed, and how are they handled before blasting?
2. Which exact areas are shot blasted, and which need edge or detail tools?
3. What profile and surface condition does the specified system require?
4. How is the prepared surface compared with that requirement?
5. How are dust, media, debris, and waste contained and removed?
6. Which cracks, joints, patches, and spalls are repaired, and when?
7. Who inspects the floor, and what causes work to pause?
8. How is the prepared floor protected from traffic, water, oil, and dust before coating?

Compare that answer with a [diamond-grinding preparation plan](/resources/diamond-grinding-concrete-preparation/) rather than assuming one machine is categorically better. Access, substrate, existing material, specified profile, detailing, dust controls, and logistics determine the practical choice.
