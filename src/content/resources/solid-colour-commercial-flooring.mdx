A solid-colour floor can be visually quiet, easy to coordinate with a brand palette, and useful for defining work zones. It can also make dust, tire marks, repairs, surface variation, or wear more visible. The sensible choice comes from testing colour in the actual space and specifying the complete floor system behind it.

For local scope options, see our [commercial epoxy flooring](/services/commercial-epoxy-flooring).

The colour is the visible decision. Concrete condition, preparation, repairs, layer compatibility, texture, environmental limits, and return to use are the performance decisions.

## Read the room before opening the fan deck

Walk the facility while it operates. Observe daylight, overhead lighting, shadows from racking, customer sightlines, forklift paths, cleaning zones, entrances, drains, and existing markings. Edmonton winter residue may be pale on a dark floor and dark on a light one; neither colour eliminates cleaning.

Collect wall, equipment, shelving, and signage colours. Note where contrast is needed at steps, ramps, edges, or routes, but do not assume a colour combination satisfies accessibility or safety requirements without project-specific review. Emergency and traffic markings may have their own organizational or regulatory requirements.

## Approve a representative sample

Ask for a sample made with the proposed colour, texture, broadcast if any, and topcoat. A printed chart or screen is not a reliable final representation. View the sample horizontally, under operating lights, near windows and doorways, and after the type of cleaning the manufacturer permits.

Consider five questions:

1. Does the colour support the space without creating unwanted glare or visual confusion?
2. How clearly does it show common soil, lint, salt residue, and wheel marks?
3. Can the texture be cleaned with available tools?
4. How will line markings and adjacent floors read against it?
5. What will a future patch look like if colour or gloss varies?

Retain the approved sample and record product, colour designation, texture, and approval date. A sample communicates an aesthetic target; it does not prove how every batch, substrate, or lighting condition will appear.

## Expect substrate history to remain relevant

Solid colour can reveal rather than erase differences. Repairs, joints, cracks, old coating boundaries, aggregate exposure, and uneven preparation may affect texture or reflection. A thicker-looking sample does not authorize coating over unsound concrete. The proposal should map repairs and state the expected visual limits.

If the existing floor is heavily patched or appearance is critical, compare a solid finish with the [floor flake colour and design guide](/resources/floor-flake-colour-design-guide/) and the [metallic epoxy expectations guide](/resources/metallic-epoxy-expectations/). These are design conversations, not claims that one decorative approach performs better.

## Put operations ahead of the colour name

Describe traffic by actual equipment, wheels, turn points, impacts, spills, cleaning chemicals, heat, and wet exposure. Identify where stationary equipment creates point loads and where doors admit cold air or water. Give this use map to the coating manufacturer or specifier so material selection is based on documented conditions.

For Alberta workplaces, consult the official [OHS Act, Regulation and Code](https://www.alberta.ca/ohs-act-regulation-code) for applicable requirements and employer duties. A selected colour or texture does not replace hazard assessment, traffic management, housekeeping, drainage, signage, or worker instruction.

## Coordinate cleaning before approval

Ask the maintenance team to list cleaners, dilution control, pads or brushes, autoscrubbers, water temperature if relevant, and cleaning frequency. Compare that program with current manufacturer instructions. A very light or dark floor may change how quickly visible soil triggers cleaning, while texture can change the effort needed. These are operational trade-offs, not universal advantages.

Request closeout documents that name permitted routine cleaning, spill response, prohibited products, and a contact for unusual damage. Train the team before the first shift on the new floor, not after an aggressive pad changes the finish.

## Plan air, access, and neighbours

Product selection includes every repair, primer, body coat, colour coat, and topcoat. Collect current safety data sheets, then plan storage, ventilation, isolation, and communication for the actual building. Shared air systems and adjacent tenants can make a small sales-floor project a larger coordination task.

The CCOHS [indoor air quality publication](https://www.ccohs.ca/products/publications/iaq.html) describes a coordinated approach involving building systems, contaminant sources, occupants, investigation, and communication. It supports treating indoor air as a project responsibility rather than judging conditions by odour alone. Actual controls must follow applicable Alberta requirements, SDSs, and the site hazard assessment.

## Compare proposals on the same page

A complete quotation should identify:

- measured zones and work boundaries;
- preparation and concrete-repair allowances;
- treatment of cracks, joints, drains, posts, and transitions;
- complete named layer sequence;
- colour and representative sample approval;
- texture and line-marking coordination;
- environmental monitoring and ventilation responsibility;
- inspection checkpoints and concealed-condition process;
- restrictions for foot traffic, equipment, cleaning, and normal use;
- maintenance and repair documentation at handover.

Ask how phase lines will look and where they will fall. If future additions are likely, keep accurate product and colour records, but avoid a promise of an invisible match years later.

## Decide with a small mock-up, not a big assumption

Where appearance carries business risk, a site mock-up can show colour, light, texture, and preparation effects more clearly than disconnected chips. Establish whether it is retained, incorporated, or removed and who signs off. If the mock-up reveals unacceptable glare, soil visibility, or cleaning difficulty, adjust the specification before full application.

Solid colour is a design choice inside a technical system. It does not establish suitability, safety, durability, or code compliance by itself.
