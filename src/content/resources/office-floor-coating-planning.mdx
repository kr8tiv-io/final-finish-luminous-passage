## Start with a day in the office

Before discussing products, map how the workplace functions from opening to close. Mark entrances, exits, reception, washrooms, lunch areas, meeting rooms, server or electrical rooms, delivery paths, accessible routes, and spaces that cannot close together. Note after-hours occupants such as cleaners, security, and maintenance staff.

For local scope options, see our [commercial epoxy flooring](/services/commercial-epoxy-flooring).

Then identify what flooring work changes: noise during preparation, dust risk, odour or vapour controls, furniture relocation, blocked paths, power needs, and cure protection. A promise of “low disruption” has no meaning until these constraints are written.

## Assign decisions before the work

Name one facility contact and one contractor contact for every shift. Define who can release an area, authorize building-system changes, approve newly found repairs, communicate with occupants, and reopen the space. Include property management and the landlord where leases divide those responsibilities.

The plan should align with the employer's occupational-health and emergency procedures. Flooring contractors can provide product and task information, but they should not invent the office's evacuation, accommodation, security, or ventilation policy.

## Phase by functional zone

Phasing is more useful than simply dividing square footage. A phase must have a safe route around it, a place for furniture, access for equipment, a defined stopping edge, and enough time for every layer and restriction.

For each phase, record:

- last time occupants may enter;
- furniture, records, and IT equipment to move;
- preparation and application window;
- barrier and sign locations;
- available exits, washrooms, and accessible routes;
- ventilation and security arrangements;
- inspection, cleaning, and reopening authority;
- contingency if conditions delay cure.

The [commercial downtime planning guide](/resources/commercial-flooring-downtime-planning/) helps turn these fields into an operational schedule.

## Control dust before discussing odour

Mechanical preparation can affect nearby work through noise and dust. Ask for the equipment, collection method, edge-work approach, barrier plan, waste route, and cleanup acceptance. Protect sensitive electronics and records according to facility requirements; do not assume a closed office door provides adequate containment.

Trace building airflow with facilities personnel. Identify supply and return grilles, shared air handlers, stairwells, lifts, and door pressure. Only authorized personnel should alter HVAC operation.

## Treat air claims carefully

Health Canada identifies paints, varnishes, glues, and other products as possible [sources of volatile organic compounds](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/indoor-air-contaminants/volatile-organic-compounds.html) and advises following label directions and using proper ventilation. Request current safety and technical data sheets for every repair material, primer, coating, cleaner, and solvent. Have the workplace's qualified reviewer determine controls.

“Low odour” does not establish low risk, and a smell check does not establish readiness. [Health Canada's indoor-air guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/improve-indoor-air-quality-in-your-home.html) recommends increased ventilation during renovation work; the facility team must decide how that general advice applies to the actual building and outdoor conditions.

## Inventory objects before they become change orders

List desks, partitions, millwork, rolling files, safes, appliances, floor outlets, cables, door stops, baseboards, and equipment fixed to the slab. Identify who disconnects, moves, stores, protects, and reinstalls each item. Photograph walls and furniture before movement.

Confirm whether the finish extends beneath movable items, fixed cabinetry, and modular partitions. Define treatment at carpet, tile, lifts, thresholds, and base. A clean transition detail often matters more to daily use than the centre of the room.

## Reopening needs multiple criteria

Do not base reopening solely on a surface feeling dry. Product documents may distinguish foot traffic, furniture, rolling loads, cleaning, and chemical exposure. Add facility criteria: barriers removed, routes restored, housekeeping completed, ventilation returned to approved operation, signs removed, alarms or security checked, and punch-list items isolated.

The person who opens the office should have a short signed checklist, not a verbal message. Keep maintenance instructions and the installed-system record available to cleaners and facilities staff.

## Make the proposal operational

A useful office proposal names preparation, repairs, system layers, site-condition limits, noise and dust controls, air-information responsibilities, phases, furniture scope, transitions, cleaning, waste, return-to-use stages, and change process. Unknown substrate conditions should be identified rather than hidden.

## Communicate by audience

Occupants need simple information: affected area, dates, alternate routes, expected noise, who to contact, and what not to move. Security needs authorized names, access hours, keys, alarms, and delivery rules. Cleaners need barriers, approved products, and reopening status. Building operations needs power, ventilation, loading, waste, and emergency details.

Prepare each notice from the same phase plan so instructions do not conflict. Update the revision date when timing changes and remove obsolete signs. A message saying “floor work this weekend” is not enough for someone arriving to find an accessible route closed.

## Close each phase before starting another

Use a turnover walk to record edges, transitions, colour, texture, cleanliness, access restoration, and outstanding items. Photograph the completed area before furniture returns. If a defect or delay affects the next phase, revise the sequence instead of allowing the same issue to propagate.

Hold a brief post-project review with facilities and cleaning staff. Capture what affected occupants, which controls worked, and which maintenance questions remain. That record improves later recoats or additional floors in the building.

Archive phase maps, product records, notices, and maintenance instructions together so facilities teams do not reconstruct the job from invoices alone.
