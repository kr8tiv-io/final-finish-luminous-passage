## Can you coat a heated garage floor?

Yes. Garages with in-floor heat are common around Edmonton, and a coating works well on them. A heated slab changes three things about the job: how deep anything can safely cut into the concrete, how warm the slab is while the coating goes down, and how moisture moves through it. None of them is a reason not to coat. All three are reasons to mention the heat before anyone writes a quote.

## Where the tubing is, and why it matters

Hydronic in-floor heat runs warm water through plastic tubing cast into the slab. The tubing sits well below the surface, and surface preparation removes only a thin layer, so normal diamond grinding does not reach it. The risk comes from deeper work: cutting out a crack, removing a broken section, drilling for anchors or posts, or cutting a new joint.

Before any of that, the tubing has to be located. If you have the layout drawings from construction, share them. If not, running the heat and scanning the floor with a thermal camera shows the tubing as warm lines. Mark them before any deep repair starts.

Heated slabs crack and curl for the same reasons other slabs do. The [ACI guide to floor and slab construction](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=302115) explains how slabs are built and why they move, which helps when you are deciding which cracks are cosmetic.

## Slab temperature during the install

Coatings are chemistry, and temperature changes how long a mix stays workable and how it cures. Manufacturers publish the temperature range each product is designed for. The [product data for one widely used epoxy floor coating](https://usa.sika.com/en/construction-products/flooring/flooring-products/epoxy-coatings/sikafloor-217.html), for example, gives cure times for air and slab temperatures between 10 and 30 °C.

A heated slab can sit near the warm end of a range like that, and it heats unevenly: warmer over the tubing, cooler between the runs. Concrete that is too warm shortens working time and can cause bubbles and patchy curing. So the heat is usually turned down ahead of the install and brought back up gradually afterward. How far ahead, and how gradually, depends on the product and the slab. Ask your installer to name the plan.

Garages heated by unit heaters or forced air are different. They warm the air, not the slab, and in cold months the concrete can stay much colder than the room. The [cold-weather guide](/resources/cold-weather-floor-coating-edmonton/) covers that case, and the [cure basics guide](/resources/how-epoxy-floor-coatings-cure/) explains the chemistry.

## Moisture and warm concrete

Moisture moves from warm to cool and from wet to dry. A heated slab over cold ground does not behave like an unheated one, and the coating is the layer that moisture meets. The same product data tells installers to make sure there is no vapour drive at the time of application.

A moisture test answers the question with a number. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) describes testing relative humidity inside the slab, and the product's documents say what reading it accepts. A test is worth doing when the slab is new, has a history of dampness, or shows white deposits. Ask how and when the test will be run on a heated floor. The [moisture testing guide](/resources/concrete-moisture-testing-floor-coatings/) explains the methods.

## Cracks, joints, and a slab that warms and cools

A heated slab expands a little when it warms and shrinks when it cools, every heating season. Control joints give that movement somewhere to go. Before coating, decide with your contractor which joints are filled and coated over, and which are kept as joints so the slab can still move. A crack that opens and closes with the seasons behaves like a joint and should be treated like one.

Cosmetic cracks that are stable are filled as part of the preparation. If a crack has grown since the heat was installed, mention it. It may be a sign of movement worth understanding before the floor is coated.

## Floor drains and meltwater

Heated garages often have a floor drain or a trench drain, because the floor melts snow faster than it can evaporate. The coating should finish cleanly at the drain so water runs into it, not under the coating's edge. If water pools short of the drain today, that low spot is worth fixing before the floor is coated; the [levelling guide](/resources/levelling-concrete-before-coating/) explains how.

## Why heated floors are worth coating

A heated garage floor dries fast. Snow melts off the car, the water evaporates, and the road salt stays behind. On bare concrete that residue works into the surface. [Alberta Infrastructure's bulletin on de-icing chemicals](https://www.alberta.ca/system/files/custom_downloaded_images/tr-tb-001a.pdf) describes how de-icers and saturation damage concrete over time. A coated floor keeps the salt on top, where a mop can take it.

A warm, coated floor is also a better place to work, and it cleans up quickly after a winter of slush. The [winter salt guide](/resources/winter-salt-concrete-floor-damage/) covers the damage patterns on uncoated slabs.

## What to tell your contractor, and what to ask

Tell them:

- That the floor has in-floor heat, and what kind.
- Where the tubing drawings are, if you have them.
- The usual winter thermostat setting.
- Any leaks, heating repairs, or cracks that have grown.
- Whether the slab is new.

Ask them:

- How will the slab temperature be managed before, during, and after the install?
- Which repairs involve cutting or drilling, and how will the tubing be located first?
- Is a moisture test planned, and under what conditions?
