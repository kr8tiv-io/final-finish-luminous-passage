## The chemistry name is only the first line

Polyaspartic products are often discussed as if the category were a complete floor specification. It is not. A buyer still needs the exact product, substrate assessment, preparation, repair plan, layer sequence, application conditions, decorative finish, maintenance instructions, and return-to-use stages.

For local scope options, see our [epoxy flooring services](/services/).

Use neutral language when comparing options. Claims such as “better than epoxy,” “one-day,” or “UV proof” compress many variables into a slogan. Ask which current technical document supports the claim for the named product and the proposed use.

## Read the proposal from concrete upward

Have the contractor list every layer: repairs, primer if used, base or body coat, flake or aggregate, and topcoat. Polyaspartic material may be used in one or several positions; do not assume the same chemistry throughout.

For each layer, record manufacturer, product, intended function, application amount or thickness, recoat requirements, and compatibility with the layers around it. If manufacturers are mixed, ask who accepted that interface in writing. The [hybrid flooring guide](/resources/hybrid-flooring-systems-neutral-guide/) provides a system-stack worksheet.

## Concrete condition still sets the starting point

Fast application does not shorten assessment. Look for weak concrete, contamination, old coatings, cracks, joints, repairs, drainage, and moisture indicators. Ask how the surface will be cleaned and mechanically prepared, what profile the product requires, and how edges and drains are treated.

The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) connects evaluation, surface preparation, repair, material selection, and quality control. That sequence is useful regardless of coating chemistry.

If moisture evaluation is relevant, specify the test and product limit. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) defines an in-situ relative-humidity method for concrete slabs; it does not set one acceptable result for every polyaspartic product or layer. Ask whether the proposed test, slab condition, and result meet the complete manufacturer's system requirements.

## A faster reaction changes planning

Some products in this category may have relatively short working and recoat periods under stated conditions. That can be useful for scheduling, but it demands crew, batch, tool, and transition planning. Ask how application time changes with material, slab, and ambient temperature and how the crew prevents seams or missed edges.

The proposal should state who records mixing and application times, temperature, humidity, and any other conditions required by the data sheet. It should also explain what happens if a recoat window is missed. Mechanical preparation or another documented step may be required; the answer must come from the current product instructions.

A quick foot-traffic milestone does not automatically authorize furniture, mats, vehicles, cleaning, or chemical exposure. Request each stage separately and establish what event starts the clock.

## Appearance comes from more than resin

In a flake floor, base colour, chip blend, broadcast density, clear-film build, sheen, and traction media shape the result. Approve a labelled physical sample and record exact codes. Ask whether flakes are lightly scattered or fully broadcast and how the intended texture is created.

For exposed sunlight near overhead doors or windows, ask for product-specific colour and gloss information rather than category claims. A topcoat's documented weathering or light-exposure properties do not automatically describe materials beneath it or guarantee no change.

## Compare real exposures

List what the floor will meet: hot tires, tracked de-icing residue, grit, welding, rolling loads, cleaners, oils, sunlight, or wet footwear. Then ask which manufacturer test data and limitations are relevant. Do not translate one laboratory result into a universal lifespan or warranty.

Traction is also system- and condition-specific. Texture, contaminants, footwear, slope, cleaning, and wear interact. Ask how a selected traction additive affects appearance and cleaning rather than accepting “non-slip” as a product feature.

## Maintenance and repair should be decided now

Request approved cleaners, tools, spill response, mat restrictions, inspection points, and recoat preparation. Ask how a scratch, chip, or localized delamination is repaired and where a visually honest boundary can fall. Decorative flake and aged sheen may make invisible repair unrealistic.

Keep the full installed-system record, not only the topcoat name. A future repair professional needs every layer, application date, and maintenance history.

## Put category claims through a three-part test

When a proposal makes a performance statement, ask for three linked items: the named product and test or technical document; the project condition that makes the information relevant; and the limitation or maintenance requirement that accompanies it. If any part is missing, the claim is not ready to guide selection.

For example, a published result for one topcoat does not automatically describe the primer, repair layer, decorative broadcast, or concrete bond. A stated application range does not prove that every part of an Edmonton garage slab will remain within it. The contractor's quality-control plan closes the gap between product information and field conditions.

Before signing, compare the polyaspartic proposal against at least one complete alternative using identical rows for preparation, layers, closure, use, maintenance, repair, documentation, and warranty. This keeps the decision about the floor rather than the marketing category.

Keep the site photographs and final sample too.

Replace unsupported shorthand in the proposal with exact product language, then keep the approved sample, colour record, maintenance guide, comparison, and cited documents in the selection record.
