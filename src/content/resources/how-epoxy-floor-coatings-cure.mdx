## Cure is chemistry, not simply drying

Two-part epoxy coatings develop through a chemical reaction after resin and hardener are combined. That makes cure different from a water puddle evaporating. The mix ratio, mixing method, material temperature, slab temperature, room conditions, film build, and elapsed time can all affect the process described by the product manufacturer.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

A surface that no longer feels tacky may still have restrictions. “Dry to touch,” “recoat,” “light foot traffic,” and “full service” are different milestones. Use only the terms and conditions in the current technical data sheet for every layer in the actual system.

## The working clock starts at mixing

Once components are mixed, the applicator has a limited working period defined by the product document. Material held in a container may behave differently from material spread across a floor because the mass and heat flow differ. This is why a crew plans batch size, labour, tools, edges, and transitions before mixing.

Ask the proposal to identify exact products and who verifies component ratio, mixing time, induction step if any, batch sequence, and disposal of material beyond its usable period. Adding unapproved solvent or changing the ratio to alter workability is not an acceptable improvisation.

## Conditions belong in the installation record

“Room temperature” is too vague. The contractor should follow the manufacturer's requirements for ambient temperature, substrate temperature, relative humidity, ventilation, and any relationship to dew point. Record readings at the times and locations the manufacturer requires.

Edmonton weather matters indirectly: an open overhead door, a cold slab near the entrance, or a heater can create different conditions across one garage. The answer is measurement and control, not a seasonal slogan. Discuss how heat is supplied, how combustion or airflow could affect the work, and what happens if conditions leave the allowed range.

Concrete moisture is a separate issue from air humidity. If slab-moisture testing is part of the specification, state the method and acceptance limits. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) defines in-situ relative-humidity testing for slabs, while the selected coating manufacturer determines whether and how such results are accepted for its system.

## Layer-to-layer timing is its own stage

Multi-layer floors may include repair material, primer, body coat, decorative media, and topcoat. Each interface can have minimum and maximum recoat conditions. Recoating too early or too late may require a different response under the manufacturer's instructions.

For each layer, record product, batch if available, mixing time, application start and finish, measured conditions, and planned next step. If the recoat window is missed, stop and obtain the documented preparation method rather than hiding the deviation. The guide to [floor-coating recoat windows](/resources/floor-coating-recoat-windows/) explains how to ask for that decision in writing.

The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) emphasizes evaluation, material selection, preparation, application, and quality control as connected work. That perspective is useful here: cure does not rescue weak concrete, contamination, or incompatible layers.

## Protect the developing floor

Create a no-entry zone that includes people who may not know the schedule: household members, cleaners, delivery workers, pets, and other trades. Prevent dust, water, insects, leaves, and windblown debris from reaching wet material. Do not place cardboard, plastic, mats, tape, tools, or furniture on the finish unless the installer confirms the current stage permits it.

If work occurs in an attached garage or occupied building, plan ventilation and access with the applicable safety data sheets and building requirements. Cure and ventilation are related planning issues, but an odour check is not a clearance test.

## Return to use is a ladder

Ask for separate written times and conditions for:

1. installer access for the next layer;
2. inspection or light foot traffic;
3. moving furniture or equipment;
4. placing mats or non-breathable coverings;
5. cleaning or water exposure;
6. vehicle, rolling-load, or chemical service.

Record what “time zero” means for each milestone—completion of the relevant coat, the final coat, or another defined event. If site conditions differ from those used in the published data, ask the manufacturer or qualified installer how the schedule changes. The [furniture return guide](/resources/when-to-move-furniture-back-coated-floor/) provides a practical move-in checklist.

## If the cure appears abnormal

Do not add heat, solvent, powder, or another coat as a guess. Restrict access, photograph the affected area, record conditions and timing, and contact the installer. Sticky areas, soft film, uneven gloss, or unusual marks can have more than one cause. Preserve leftover labels and project records.

## Turn the data sheet into a homeowner schedule

Before installation, ask the contractor to translate the technical milestones into dated, conditional instructions without changing their meaning. The handover note might say which coat was completed and when, what conditions were recorded, the earliest permitted activity for each use, and whom to contact before advancing if the floor looks or feels unusual.

Keep that note at the entrance so a delivery driver, cleaner, or family member does not unknowingly enter. If a milestone depends on maintained conditions, continue monitoring as the installer directs rather than assuming the application reading applies forever.

For phased work, post the current phase and coat beside the schedule so one area's restriction is not casually applied to another. There is no responsible universal answer for how many hours an “epoxy floor” needs because formulations, layers, conditions, and intended use vary.
