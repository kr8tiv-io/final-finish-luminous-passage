## What an auto shop floor has to survive

A service bay floor takes more than almost any other: motor oil, brake fluid, coolant, battery acid, solvents, degreasers, hot tires, jack stands, lift posts, creepers, and dropped tools, every day. A coating turns bare, stained concrete into a surface that sheds fluids, cleans quickly, and reflects light. Getting there means choosing the system for those exposures and planning the work around the bays. Final Finish's commercial work covers surface preparation, crack filling, and anti-slip finishes.

## Fluids and chemical resistance

Every coating resists some chemicals better than others. Brake fluid and some solvents are harder on coatings than water-based fluids. The product documents include chemical resistance information, often as a table of substances and exposure times. Ask for it, check it against what your shop actually uses, and plan for prompt cleanup. A spill wiped up in minutes is a different exposure from a puddle left over a weekend.

Keep safety data sheets for the coating products as well as the shop's own chemicals. The [CCOHS guide to safety data sheets](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/chemicals/whmis_ghs/sds.html) explains what they contain, and Alberta's [OHS Code on chemical hazards](https://search-ohs-laws.alberta.ca/legislation/occupational-health-and-safety-code/part-4-chemical-hazards-biological-hazards-and-harmful-substances/) sets the workplace rules.

## Hot tires, lifts, and point loads

- **Hot tires.** Vehicles coming in off hot pavement can soften some top coats and leave marks. The top coat choice matters most here. The [hot-tire pickup guide](/resources/hot-tire-pickup-epoxy/) explains why.
- **Lifts.** Two-post and four-post lift bases concentrate load and are anchored to the slab. Coating around anchored equipment needs a plan: coat before the lift goes in, or cut in neatly around the base.
- **Jack stands and creepers.** Point loads and hard casters wear top coats. Plates under stands help.
- **Dropped tools.** Chips happen. A flake floor hides them better than a light solid colour, and small chips can be repaired.

## Washdown, detailing, and grip

Detailing bays and wash areas are wet most of the day, and wet, glossy coatings can be slippery. The [CCOHS guidance on preventing slips, trips, and falls](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/falls.html) notes that most workplace falls happen on level floors, from slips and trips. An anti-slip top coat in wet zones and walkways adds grip.

Drains and trench drains need clean terminations, and water should run toward them, not away. The [slip resistance guide](/resources/slip-resistance-floor-coatings/) covers texture options for wet floors.

## Light floors for bright bays

A light solid colour bounces light back up under vehicles and into the bay, which helps technicians see. The trade-off is that light floors show tire marks and fluid stains sooner. Many shops pair a light colour in the work zones with a darker colour or flake in the traffic lanes. Bay lines and safety markings can be part of the system. The [solid-colour commercial guide](/resources/solid-colour-commercial-flooring/) has more on colour choices.

## Oil already in the concrete

An older shop floor has years of drips soaked into it. Coating over oil-soaked concrete is where many shop floors fail, because the coating cannot bond to a surface that keeps bleeding oil back up. The preparation has to deal with it: degreasing, grinding, and in heavily soaked spots, further treatment or repair until the concrete is sound and clean. Ask how contaminated areas will be handled and how the crew will judge them ready. The [oil stain guide](/resources/remove-oil-stains-before-floor-coating/) goes into the options.

## The customer side of the counter

A shop floor is also part of what customers see. A clean, bright floor in the bays and the waiting area tells people the work is done with care. If the shop has a showroom or reception, it can carry a more decorative finish than the service bays, while the bays get the grip and top coat they need.

## Keeping it looking new

Grit is what dulls a coated floor, so sweeping or scrubbing it out daily does more than anything else. Clean fluids up as they happen, use a neutral cleaner rather than harsh solvents for routine mopping, and keep an eye on the busiest bays. A worn top coat can usually be renewed before the wear reaches the layers below. The [scratch care guide](/resources/scratch-maintenance-coated-floor/) covers routine upkeep.

## Downtime is a plan, not a hope

A closed bay is not billing. Coating work needs empty bays, time for preparation, and time to cure before vehicles return. Plan it around your quietest days, and phase it if bays can be taken out of service a few at a time.

Ask for the schedule in writing: when each bay goes down, when it can be walked on, and when it takes vehicles. Our [commercial downtime guide](/resources/commercial-flooring-downtime-planning/) walks through it.
