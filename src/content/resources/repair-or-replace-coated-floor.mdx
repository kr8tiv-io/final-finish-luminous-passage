The choice between repairing and replacing a coated floor is rarely determined by the size of the ugly spot alone. A small blister can point to a broader moisture or adhesion issue; a large worn path may be serviceable if the coating remains bonded and the wear mechanism is understood. The useful question is: what failed, why did it fail, and where does sound material begin?

That requires an assessment of both the visible coating and the concrete beneath it. A repair is a system decision, not just colour matching.

## First, classify what you can see

Walk the floor in consistent light and mark each type of concern separately. Do not group every imperfection under “peeling.” Record scratches, loss of gloss, worn texture, impact chips, lifting edges, hollow-sounding zones, blisters, cracks, dampness, white deposits, and failed patches. Photographs should include a wide view and a close view with location notes.

Add the age of the observed change if known, recent leaks or flooding, cleaning chemicals, vehicle or cart traffic, and any prior repair. This condition map becomes the basis for a scope that can be compared across proposals.

## A local repair needs a sound boundary

Spot repair is most credible when damage is localized, the cause has stopped, surrounding coating is sound enough to prepare, and the new repair materials are compatible with the existing system. The edge matters: if preparation keeps revealing loose material, the repair boundary may need to expand.

The [ACI PRC-546 concrete repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) covers evaluation, repair, surface preparation, protective systems, and quality control. That primary-source framework supports assessing deterioration before selecting a repair material. It does not turn an unknown old coating into a verified compatible base; product identification and current manufacturer instructions remain necessary.

Appearance also deserves an honest conversation. Even a technically suitable patch can remain visible because of colour, gloss, texture, aggregate exposure, ageing, or lighting. If a seamless appearance matters, a larger recoat area may be considered even when the functional repair is smaller. That is an aesthetic scope choice, not proof the entire coating has failed.

## Signs that point toward broader removal

Replacement or extensive removal deserves discussion when failure is repeated or widespread, the old coating is poorly bonded across multiple zones, the system is unknown and compatibility cannot be established, contamination extends beyond a local area, or repairs would create a patchwork that does not meet operational needs.

Cracking may also change the decision. A coating cannot diagnose structural movement, stop an active leak, or make unstable concrete sound. Significant movement, unexplained displacement, active water entry, or deteriorated concrete should be referred to an appropriately qualified professional before a decorative replacement scope is finalized.

If the issue appears limited to scuffs or wear, start with the [scratch-maintenance guide for coated floors](/resources/scratch-maintenance-coated-floor/) rather than assuming removal is necessary. If the coating is lifting, the [floor-coating site-assessment checklist](/resources/floor-coating-site-assessment-checklist/) provides a useful record to bring to the visit.

## Moisture must be considered separately

Surface symptoms can suggest moisture, but they do not establish its source or quantity. Plumbing leaks, exterior drainage, joints, vapour movement, and cleaning practices can produce different patterns. A handheld reading, when used, should not be presented as equivalent to every standardized slab test.

[ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) sets out a method for measuring relative humidity in concrete floor slabs using in-situ probes. If the replacement system’s manufacturer calls for this method, project documents should identify the test edition, locations, conditioning, results, and product-specific acceptance limit. Moisture testing is evidence for a system decision; it is not a generic promise that a repair will succeed.

## Compare three possible scopes

### Stabilize and repair

Define what is removed, how the boundary is checked, how concrete is repaired, and which layers are reinstated. Include the likely visual difference and the process if loose coating extends farther than expected.

### Prepare and recoat a larger area

This may help unify appearance or renew a worn surface, but only if the retained coating is a permitted substrate. The proposal should state how adhesion, cleanliness, existing texture, and recoat compatibility are evaluated.

### Remove and replace the system

State the removal method, edge and joint treatment, concrete repairs, moisture requirements, new layer sequence, ventilation controls, and staged return to service. “Full replacement” should not conceal exclusions such as inaccessible areas or deeper concrete repair.

## Questions worth asking before authorizing work

Ask the contractor to show the observations behind the recommendation:

1. Where is the coating considered sound, and how was that evaluated?
2. What is the likely failure mechanism, and has its source been addressed?
3. Is the existing product known? Which manufacturer documents support compatibility?
4. How will concealed deterioration change the scope?
5. Which joints and cracks are included, and which require separate review?
6. What visual differences should be expected after a local repair?
7. What are the written environmental, cure, cleaning, and return-to-service restrictions?

Avoid choosing solely by the smallest initial area or the broadest recommendation. The strongest planning document connects observed conditions to preparation, materials, checkpoints, and exclusions. Because this resource cannot inspect the floor or verify the existing system, it remains a review-gated guide rather than a claim that any particular Edmonton floor should be repaired or replaced.

Keep the photographs, inspection notes, old product records, and accepted scope after the work. If another area changes later, that file makes it easier to compare patterns and determine whether the new concern is local or part of the same mechanism.
