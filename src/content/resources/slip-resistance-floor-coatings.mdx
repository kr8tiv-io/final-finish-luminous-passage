“Non-slip” is not a useful standalone specification. A floor’s real conditions change with water, oil, grease, dust, footwear, slope, wear, cleaning residue, and behaviour. Texture can be part of a safer-floor strategy, but it cannot make every contaminant, task, or user safe.

For Edmonton properties, start by mapping the winter pathway. Snow and de-icer arrive at entrances, melt near parked vehicles or vestibules, and can move farther on footwear and wheels. In workplaces, add process liquids, wash areas, kitchens, loading docks, ramps, and changes between floor materials.

## Write an exposure map

For each zone, record when it is dry or wet, what the contaminant is, how quickly spills are removed, footwear or wheels, direction of travel, slope, drainage, lighting, and cleaning method. Note trips and near misses without treating their absence as proof that the surface is suitable.

Separate permanent and operational controls. Drainage, a specified texture, and transitions may be built into the floor. Mats, spill response, footwear, warning signs, traffic rules, and housekeeping require ongoing ownership. If one control fails, the plan should not depend on a marketing label.

The Government of Alberta’s [OHS Act, Regulation and Code page](https://www.alberta.ca/ohs-act-regulation-code) provides the official legislation and search tools for confirming applicable workplace duties. Use it when the floor is part of a workplace hazard assessment. A supplier’s description does not replace the employer’s obligations or a competent, site-specific review.

## Texture has a maintenance cost

Adding aggregate or creating a more pronounced texture can change how the surface feels and how soil is retained. Cleaning tools must reach the texture without damaging it. A finish that cannot be maintained with available staff, chemicals, drainage, and equipment may perform differently in use than a clean sample.

Bring the cleaning team into selection. Ask them to test the intended mop, brush, vacuum, or autoscrubber on a representative area. Compare cleaners and pads with current manufacturer guidance. Discuss how grease, detergent film, or winter residue will be removed and where wash water goes.

The [restaurant floor planning guide](/resources/restaurant-floor-coating-planning/) shows how this trade-off affects food-service operations. For garages, the [winter salt and concrete-floor guide](/resources/winter-salt-concrete-floor-damage/) covers seasonal residue and substrate observations.

## Ask what a reported number actually means

Slip-related test results are meaningful only with the named method, apparatus, surface condition, contaminant, direction, specimen preparation, and reporting rules. Numbers from different methods or conditions should not be treated as directly interchangeable. A value from a clean laboratory sample does not certify the installed floor under every spill, wear state, or cleaning routine.

If a project requires a measured criterion, involve a qualified specifier. Put the test method, condition, locations, timing, acceptance process, and responsible party in the project documents. Ask how field texture will be kept consistent at edges, repairs, drains, and phase lines. Do not publish a numeric threshold unless the applicable authority, standard, and project context have been verified.

## Build a sample that answers real questions

A useful mock-up represents the proposed layer sequence, aggregate, application, and topcoat. Review it under actual light with typical footwear. If the specifier approves a wet or contaminated evaluation, control it safely and follow the selected method; do not create an improvised slip test in a public route.

The sample should answer:

- Is the texture visually acceptable across the expected colour?
- Can the planned cleaning tools remove soil from it?
- Are barefoot users, carts, or small wheels relevant?
- How will the finish transition to adjacent flooring?
- Will repairs be visibly or texturally different?

Keep the approved sample and record who accepted it. It is a communication tool, not a guarantee of all future conditions.

## Indoor-air planning still belongs in the scope

Texture may be only one part of a multi-layer application. Primers, binders, topcoats, repairs, and cleaners each have current safety and product data. The CCOHS official [indoor air quality publication](https://www.ccohs.ca/products/publications/iaq.html) explains why building systems, contaminant sources, occupants, investigation, and communication must be considered together. Use that framework for work near occupied spaces while following applicable Alberta requirements and each SDS.

## Inspect the floor after handover

Reopening is the beginning of operational control, not the end. Check entrances, drains, turns, ramps, transitions, and repaired areas. Confirm mats lie flat, spill materials are stocked, staff know the cleaning and reporting process, and the approved cleaner and tools are available. Reassess after seasonal change or a cleaning-program change.

If a zone becomes polished, contaminated, damaged, or unexpectedly difficult to clean, document it and respond promptly. A local repair or added texture must remain compatible with the installed system and may change appearance.

## A better quotation avoids blanket promises

Ask each bidder to name the observed exposure, proposed texture, full layer sequence, sample process, cleaning requirements, inspection points, transitions, and limits. “Slip resistant” without conditions is not enough. Neither is a disclaimer that shifts all responsibility to the owner while leaving the specified texture undefined.

No article or coating can certify a floor safe for every user and event.
