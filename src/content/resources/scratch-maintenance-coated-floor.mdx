A mark on a coated floor is not automatically a scratch, and every scratch does not need a repair coating. The safest first move is to slow down, identify the type of damage, and consult the maintenance instructions for the installed system. Aggressive trial-and-error cleaning can turn a removable transfer mark into a permanent dull patch.

## Sort the mark before choosing a response

Look across the floor under low-angle and overhead light. A dark line that sits on the surface may be rubber, plastic, or metal transfer. A pale line may be a change in gloss. A groove that catches a clean fingernail may extend into a coating layer. A chip with exposed broadcast media or concrete is a different condition again.

Photograph the area, then record likely causes: chair legs, wheeled bins, pallets, jacks, vehicle grit, dragged equipment, snow-clearing tools, or a recent cleaning change. In an Edmonton entrance or garage, winter sand and road debris often act as loose abrasive under tires, casters, and footwear. Removing the source is part of maintenance.

Do not use a fingernail test as a technical depth measurement, and do not sand a finished floor simply to investigate. If the product and layer sequence are unknown, treat the system as unknown until records or a qualified assessment establish otherwise.

## Begin with the least aggressive permitted cleaning

Find the original installer documents, product names, colour, texture, batch information if available, and manufacturer maintenance instructions. Follow the specified dilution, tools, rinsing, and dwell limits for a suitable cleaner. Test in a discreet area first and allow it to dry before judging gloss or colour.

Avoid assuming that a household abrasive pad, solvent, concentrated degreaser, polishing compound, or pressure washer is compatible. A product may remove the mark while softening, scratching, discolouring, or changing the texture of the finish. Never mix cleaning chemicals. Follow label and safety-data-sheet precautions and prevent runoff from reaching an inappropriate drain.

If ordinary maintenance does not remove the mark, stop before escalating. That is the point to send photographs and the product record to the installer or coating manufacturer.

## Use a simple damage ladder

### Transfer or residue

Confirm the substance if possible and use only a permitted cleaning method. Review wheels, mats, footwear, and housekeeping so it does not return.

### Surface scuff or gloss change

Cleaning may not restore the original reflection. Ask whether the manufacturer allows a maintenance treatment and whether it would change the surrounding area. A local polished spot can be more noticeable than the initial scuff.

### Scratch within the finish

Ask whether a localized touch-up is permitted. Colour, sheen, texture, ageing, and application method can make the repair visible. Masking a small rectangle may create a sharp edge; feathering may create a larger gloss difference. Appearance expectations belong in writing before work starts.

### Cut, chip, or exposed concrete

Keep contaminants and water out of the damaged area and arrange an assessment. The [ACI concrete repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) covers condition evaluation, repair, preparation, protective systems, and quality control. That framework supports inspecting the substrate and remaining layers before rebuilding a damaged system. The current coating and repair manufacturers still determine compatibility and application requirements.

### Repeated or widespread damage

A wear lane may indicate the cleaning program, grit control, wheels, point loads, traffic layout, or selected finish does not match use. Review the entire zone rather than repeatedly touching up the same symptom. The guide to [repairing or replacing a coated floor](/resources/repair-or-replace-coated-floor/) helps frame that broader assessment.

## Prevention starts at the door and under the load

Use entrance matting that is appropriate for the facility, keep it clean, and prevent curled edges from becoming hazards. Sweep or vacuum loose grit before wet cleaning. Fit furniture protection that the furniture and flooring manufacturers permit, and inspect it because embedded grit can turn a protective pad into an abrasive. Lift furniture instead of dragging it. Use clean, suitable wheels and plan turning paths for rolling loads.

In garages and workshops, clean tracked sand and de-icer regularly, contain oil leaks, and avoid metal snow shovels or sharp tools on the coating. Under equipment, a compatible mat, tray, or sacrificial protection may be easier to replace than a finish, but it must not trap moisture or use a backing that reacts with the coating. Confirm both manufacturers’ instructions.

Maintenance teams should record cleaner, dilution, pad or brush, equipment, frequency, and any change in appearance. That log makes patterns easier to diagnose and prevents each shift from inventing a different method.

## Moisture is usually a separate investigation

A scratch does not call for slab-moisture testing. However, lifting, blistering, dampness, or white deposits around damage may point beyond surface wear. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) is a standardized method for in-situ relative-humidity testing in concrete slabs. Whether it is relevant depends on the observed condition and the requirements of a proposed repair or replacement system. Do not treat a handheld reading or the appearance of a scratch as an equivalent result.

## Know when to call for an assessment

Seek help when a cut exposes concrete, edges continue to lift, damage follows cracks, a chemical spill is involved, the floor becomes unexpectedly slippery, or repeated repairs fail. Bring the location map, maintenance log, photos, product records, and the suspected cause.

A responsible repair proposal names the layers being treated, preparation, compatibility basis, visual limits, cure and use restrictions, and process if damage extends farther than expected.
