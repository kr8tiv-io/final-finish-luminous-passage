## Start with the room, not the colour chart

Floor flakes are small decorative chips, but their effect is architectural: they can make a floor read lighter or darker, busier or calmer. Begin with the real room. Note the walls, cabinets, overhead door, vehicles, equipment, natural light, and colour of the installed lighting.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

An Edmonton garage that looks bright on a summer afternoon can feel different under winter evening light. Carry shortlisted samples to several parts of the room and view them when the space is normally used. A phone photo helps comparison, but cameras automatically change exposure and white balance; let the eye make the final decision.

## Four controls create the appearance

People often choose only a named blend. The finished result comes from four related decisions.

**Base colour.** Gaps between chips may reveal the colour below. One blend can look different over another base.

**Blend composition.** Chip colours, sizes, and proportions establish contrast and movement. A small strong accent can read differently across a full room than in a hand sample.

**Broadcast density.** A lighter scatter leaves more base visible. A heavier broadcast makes chips dominant. Neither is automatically better; the proposal should describe the intended result and a system able to hold it.

**Clear finish and texture.** Sheen, film build, and traction media affect reflection and surface relief. These are maintenance and use decisions as well as visual ones.

Ask for a labelled sample identifying all four. Record the manufacturer's colour or blend code in the proposal because informal names can be remembered differently.

## Design for what must remain visible

A mottled, medium-contrast floor may make routine dust and small marks less visually dominant. It can also make small hardware, clear debris, or spills harder to see. A dark field may highlight pale winter residue; a light field may show tire soil. Test these observations instead of treating them as rules.

If you work on equipment, place representative fasteners on the sample and view them from standing height. At stairs, ramps, or level changes, consider whether deliberate contrast is preferable to a continuous camouflage pattern. If parking guides or safety markings matter, decide whether they are in the coating scope or separate markings applied later.

## Run a consistent sample test

Shortlist two or three options, then assess each the same way:

1. Place the sample flat on the floor, not vertically in your hand.
2. View it near the door, at the back wall, and beneath the main fixtures.
3. Set wall paint, cabinet, trim, or tile samples beside it.
4. Place a representative small object and a little clean grit on top.
5. Look from the doorway and standing height, not only close up.
6. Ask whether it still works if vehicles or wall colours change.

For an appearance-sensitive project, discuss a larger mock-up made with the proposed layers and application method. Clarify whether it is a colour reference, texture reference, or both. Random chip placement means a sample cannot predict every detail of a full floor.

## Decoration cannot fix the slab

Flakes do not bridge structural movement, correct drainage, stop moisture, or make weak concrete sound. The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) treats evaluation, repair, preparation, and protective systems as connected work. Choose colour alongside—never instead of—a concrete assessment.

Repairs may remain perceptible, especially under directional light. Ask how joints will be treated and whether they remain visible. The guide to [cracks and spalling under coatings](/resources/concrete-cracks-spalling-floor-coating/) frames that conversation. If moisture testing is specified, [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) is one recognized in-situ relative-humidity method, but the product manufacturer's current limits must govern acceptance.

## Texture, cleaning, and repairs

More surface relief may change traction but gives soil more texture around which to collect. A smoother finish may wipe more easily yet feel different when wet. Tell the contractor whether the floor sees meltwater, oily work, rolling stools, bare feet, or mobility aids. No colour or flake pattern guarantees slip resistance; contaminants, footwear, cleaning, wear, and slope matter. Use the [slip-resistance planning guide](/resources/slip-resistance-floor-coatings/) to document actual conditions.

Request care instructions for the complete finish and confirm acceptable cleaners, pads, brushes, and snow-removal tools. Retain exact codes, product names, the approved sample, and project photographs. A later repair may differ because of batch variation, aging, gloss, and random chip placement. Ask whether a natural boundary such as a joint offers the most practical repair limit.

## Put the final choice into a room record

Take one photograph of every candidate in the same location and light, then record why the chosen option won. Note any accent colour that must remain visible, the desired contrast at edges, and whether the priority is hiding routine soil or making it easy to see. This brief gives the installer more useful direction than “we liked the grey one.”

Have every decision-maker approve the same physical sample. If two adjoining rooms are coated at different times, clarify whether colour continuity or an intentional transition is expected and where the boundary falls.

Keep one dated sample with the project record so later questions refer to the same physical standard, not a screen image or memory. Before approval, have the proposal state base colour, blend code, broadcast description, clear-coat type, intended texture, and reference sample.
