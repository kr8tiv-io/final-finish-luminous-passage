## Map the exposure

## Primer is a role, not an automatic extra

A primer is the first liquid layer in some floor-coating systems. Depending on the exact product and specification, it may be intended to wet a prepared surface, support adhesion, reduce outgassing risk, create a uniform base, or address a defined moisture condition. Other published systems may not call for a separate primer.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

The useful question is not “Does primer always make a floor better?” It is “Why does this system require this primer on this slab?”

## Begin with the manufacturer's system

Request the current technical data sheets for primer and next layer. Confirm that they form a documented system and record required surface preparation, mixing, environmental limits, application amount or thickness, recoat conditions, and broadcast instructions if aggregate is used.

If products come from different manufacturers, ask who accepted their compatibility in writing. A successful bond between primer and concrete is only half the interface; the next coat must also bond to the primer.

## The slab comes before the primer

Primer does not replace cleaning or mechanical preparation. Oil, sealer, adhesive, curing compounds, laitance, weak concrete, dust, and old coating need condition-specific treatment. Ask how the crew will confirm the required surface profile and cleanliness at edges, corners, drains, cracks, and large open areas.

The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) places evaluation, repair, surface preparation, material selection, and quality control in one chain. This prevents primer from being treated as a cure-all for an unknown substrate.

Cracks, spalls, joints, and low areas also need separate decisions. Ask whether repairs occur before or after primer, how repair materials are prepared, and which instructions establish compatibility. A primer does not stop structural movement or correct drainage.

## Ask what problem the layer addresses

For a standard adhesion or wetting primer, ask what prepared-surface condition it expects and how pinholes, absorption, or dry areas are checked. For an outgassing-sensitive floor, ask how slab temperature and application timing are controlled and what evidence determines the response.

For a moisture-related primer or mitigation layer, request precise boundaries: test method, measured condition, permitted range, required film build, number of coats, quality-control checks, and exclusions. The phrase “moisture barrier” can conceal important differences.

[ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) defines in-situ relative-humidity testing for concrete slabs. It does not decide whether a primer is needed or establish one acceptance limit. The selected product documentation must connect test results to the proposed system. Review the [concrete moisture testing guide](/resources/concrete-moisture-testing-floor-coatings/) before accepting a moisture claim.

## Application quality is visible in records

A primer layer can be hidden quickly, so establish a hold point. Record the accepted prepared slab, repair status, product and batch information supplied, mix times, slab and ambient conditions, application start and finish, coverage or thickness checks required by the manufacturer, and the planned next-coat time.

Edges and porous or repaired areas may behave differently from the field. Ask how the applicator identifies absorption, pinholes, puddles, missed spots, contamination, or material beyond its working period. The response should follow the product instructions, not an improvised extra coat.

## Recoat timing protects the interface

Primer and body coat must meet within documented conditions. Ask for both minimum and maximum recoat criteria and what preparation is required if the window is exceeded. Keep dust, water, insects, workers, and other trades off the primed surface.

Do not assume a tacky feel is the universal signal. Products differ, and field temperature can change timing. The guide to [floor-coating recoat windows](/resources/floor-coating-recoat-windows/) explains how to record each interface.

## When a no-primer proposal can be legitimate

Some complete systems are designed for direct application to correctly prepared concrete. If a proposal omits primer, ask for the exact manufacturer's system document and confirm that slab conditions, preparation, application build, and intended use fit. Do not add a primer simply from habit; an unapproved layer can introduce compatibility and scheduling issues.

Likewise, do not remove a specified primer merely to save time. Compare proposals by the complete tested or documented system, not by layer count.

## Five lines the proposal should contain

The final scope should identify the primer product and purpose; substrate and repair preparation; acceptance criteria; application and recoat controls; and action for moisture, pinholes, missed windows, or other deviations. Handover records should preserve the full stack for future maintenance.

## Translate primer into a bid-comparison row

When one proposal includes primer and another does not, avoid treating the extra layer as automatic value or automatic waste. Ask both bidders to name the published system, the observed slab condition, and the function performed at the concrete interface. Then compare the complete preparation and layer sequence.

Also compare what happens after application. One primer may require a broadcast, another a particular tack or cure state, and a direct-to-concrete body coat may have its own build and timing rules. The number of layers cannot be compared without their functions.

At handover, preserve photographs of the accepted primer before it disappears, along with the product name, application time, conditions, and next-coat record promised in the scope. Those details are more useful for future investigation than the phrase “primed floor.”
