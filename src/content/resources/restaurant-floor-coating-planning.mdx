A restaurant floor project sits inside an operating system: food preparation, staff movement, cleaning, deliveries, pest control, ventilation, and reopening checks. Choosing a coating by colour or resin name misses the hard part. The planning task is to define what each zone experiences and how the business will stay safe while the work is isolated.

For local scope options, see our [commercial epoxy flooring](/services/commercial-epoxy-flooring).

For an Edmonton restaurant, winter entrances may carry water, grit, and de-icer while back-of-house areas face grease, dropped tools, and frequent cleaning. Those exposures are not interchangeable. Start with a walk-through during representative operations, then look again after cleaning if residue hides the substrate.

## Draw the floor as working zones

On a simple plan, mark the cook line, dish area, walk-in access, storage, bar, dining room, washrooms, receiving route, drains, stairs, thresholds, and exits. Add where carts turn, mats sit, water collects, fryers are serviced, and cleaning equipment is stored. Note transitions to tile, resilient flooring, bare concrete, and existing coatings.

This map should prompt questions about routine wetting, grease, actual cleaners and equipment, rolling loads, cracks, damaged drains, failed coves, and the routes that must remain available. The answers shape preparation, details, texture, and phasing; they do not justify an unsupported promise that one generic “restaurant epoxy” handles every exposure.

## Keep workplace duties in the plan

Alberta’s official page for the [Occupational Health and Safety Act, Regulation and Code](https://www.alberta.ca/ohs-act-regulation-code) provides access to current legislation and search tools. It is the primary place to confirm applicable employer, contractor, hazard-assessment, access, ventilation, and worker-protection duties. A flooring proposal does not replace the restaurant’s OHS responsibilities.

Identify who controls the area, communicates hazards, maintains barriers, and authorizes reopening. Plan dust and debris control around food and clean equipment. Remove or protect ingredients, utensils, packaging, and movable equipment according to the operation’s food-safety procedures. Confirm waste and wash-water handling. If the floor cannot be fully isolated from food activity, resolve that conflict before scheduling.

## Texture is a use-and-cleaning decision

More texture is not automatically safer in every condition. Texture interacts with footwear, slope, contamination, drainage, and the ability to remove soil. A very smooth finish may not suit a wet zone; a coarse finish that staff cannot clean effectively can create another operational problem.

Ask for a representative sample under operating lighting and, where appropriate, discuss a documented test method with the specifier. Review the [slip-resistance questions for floor coatings](/resources/slip-resistance-floor-coatings/) before approving texture by appearance alone. Keep mats, drainage, spill response, footwear, and housekeeping in the safety plan; a coating is only one control.

## Ventilation needs a named owner

Odour is not a measurement of exposure, and “low odour” is not a complete ventilation plan. Obtain current safety data sheets and product data sheets for every primer, repair material, body coat, topcoat, cleaner, and joint product. Identify where air enters and exhausts, whether air movement could carry contaminants toward occupied or food areas, and how temporary controls affect neighbouring tenants.

The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety’s official [indoor air quality publication](https://www.ccohs.ca/products/publications/iaq.html) describes a systematic approach to recognizing, investigating, and controlling indoor-air concerns. Use it to frame responsibility and communication, while following product SDSs, contractor controls, and applicable Alberta requirements for the actual materials.

## Build the shutdown from reopening backwards

A useful schedule includes more than application time. Work backwards from the first service:

1. When must customer and worker traffic resume?
2. When can fixed equipment, rolling racks, mats, and storage return under written restrictions?
3. When can the floor receive the planned cleaning and sanitizing process?
4. When will drains, coves, thresholds, and transitions be inspected?
5. When will ventilation controls change or be removed?
6. What time is reserved for extra concrete repair or an environmental delay?

Do not rely on “dry overnight.” Temperature, substrate conditions, layer sequence, and type of use matter, and current manufacturer documentation for the specified system must set restrictions. A phased project may reduce closure, but it adds edges, barriers, traffic interfaces, and handoff inspections. The [commercial flooring downtime checklist](/resources/commercial-flooring-downtime-planning/) can help operators compare sequencing in two proposals.

## Give details their own line in the scope

Ask what happens at floor-wall junctions, drains, posts, equipment legs, refrigeration penetrations, door thresholds, joints, and transitions. A cove may help a cleaning detail, but its substrate, termination, and compatibility still need specification. Existing drains should be assessed rather than simply coated around, and active joints should not disappear under a drawing.

The proposal should list areas, preparation, concrete repairs, layer sequence, texture, colours, terminations, environmental controls, protection by others, inspections, cleaning instructions, and staged return to service. It should identify exclusions and the process for concealed damage.

Before publication or purchase, Denis still needs to verify any company-specific system, schedule, warranty, credential, price, or performance statement. This review-gated guide helps a restaurant owner ask better questions; the food-appropriate solution must come from the actual site, current rules, and written product requirements.

## Prepare a reopening sign-off

Before the first shift, have operations inspect barriers removed, drains and transitions, equipment placement, floor condition, cleaning supplies, and staff routes. Confirm that the written product restrictions allow the planned loads, water, chemicals, and traffic. Record unresolved items and keep affected zones closed rather than relying on a verbal handoff. Retain the approved scope, product and safety sheets, maintenance instructions, photographs, and repair record. This closeout file gives managers and future contractors a reliable starting point when cleaning changes, equipment moves, or damage appears.
