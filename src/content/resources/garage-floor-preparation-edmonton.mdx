## Choose a question, not a slogan

## Preparation begins before equipment arrives

A sound preparation plan identifies the slab's condition, contaminants, previous treatments, repairs, joints, moisture questions, drainage, and edges before selecting a machine. Edmonton garages add a familiar use pattern: vehicles carry meltwater, grit, and de-icing residue; the doorway may be colder than the wall shared with the house; drains and parking tracks form distinct exposure zones.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

Those conditions do not dictate one system. They tell the assessor where to look.

## Read the garage zone by zone

Clear enough belongings to expose the concrete. Divide the floor into parking tracks, doorway band, perimeter, drain area, work zone, cracks, and old patches. Photograph concerns from a wide view and close up.

Look for dark or oily areas, white deposits, scaling, weak concrete, old paint or sealer, crack and patch boundaries, low spots, and water-entry points. The [Alberta Infrastructure bulletin on de-icing concrete](https://www.alberta.ca/system/files/custom_downloaded_images/tr-tb-001a.pdf) discusses de-icers, saturation, freeze-thaw exposure, and spring washing. Although written for infrastructure, it supports a useful garage principle: winter residue is a condition to remove and assess, not coat over.

## Cleaning is not profiling

Cleaning targets soluble, greasy, or loose contamination. Mechanical profiling changes the concrete surface to meet the coating specification. One cannot automatically substitute for the other. Grinding through visible oil, for example, does not by itself prove that remaining concrete is suitable.

Ask for the sequence in suspect areas: how loose material will be removed, what cleaning chemistry will be used, how residue will be extracted, and what acceptance check follows. See [oil-stain preparation questions](/resources/remove-oil-stains-before-floor-coating/) for a focused checklist.

Mechanical work may use diamond grinding, shot blasting, or another method chosen for the slab and system. The machine name alone is not a specification. Access, old material, edges, desired profile, dust control, and product instructions all matter. The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) places surface preparation within evaluation, repair, material selection, and quality control—a helpful model for the full process.

## Specify and inspect the profile

The coating manufacturer should define an acceptable prepared surface. Ask which current technical data sheet governs and how completion will be assessed. A floor can look clean while retaining weak laitance, embedded contamination, or an unsuitable profile.

Edges, corners, curbs, posts, steps, drains, and the strip under the overhead door need explicit treatment because large machines reach them differently. The proposal should name the smaller tools used and state where the coating terminates. Our [diamond-grinding guide](/resources/diamond-grinding-concrete-preparation/) expands on equipment, dust collection, and edge work.

## Repairs follow exposure

Preparation can reveal defects hidden by dirt or an old finish. Include a decision point: who reviews newly exposed cracks or unsound concrete, who authorizes added repairs, and how scope or schedule changes are approved.

Cracks should not all receive one automatic treatment. Ask whether movement is suspected and whether the cause is understood. Control and construction joints may need to keep functioning rather than being rigidly covered. Spalls require sound boundaries and a compatible repair sequence. Repair materials must fit the coating manufacturer's application and recoat requirements.

## Treat moisture as an evidence question

Past efflorescence, damp edges, dark areas, or known drainage problems may justify investigation. A coating is not a drainage repair. Ask what moisture information the manufacturer requires, which test is proposed, where tests will be placed, and how results change the system choice.

Avoid treating one casual meter reading as universal clearance. Instruments and test methods answer different questions. The [concrete moisture testing guide](/resources/concrete-moisture-testing-floor-coatings/) outlines records worth requesting: method, location, date, conditions, result, and the selected product's limit.

## Plan the work around the household

Preparation produces construction noise and dust. Confirm containment, collection equipment, ventilation, power, waste handling, and the route for equipment. Ask how the home is separated from the garage and whether the overhead door must remain open. Weather can affect that plan, so a calendar date should never override the ambient and substrate conditions in current product documents.

Discuss freezers, appliances, fixed cabinets, stairs, and objects fastened to the slab. Clarify who disconnects or moves them, where stored items and vehicles go, and whether coating beneath equipment is included.

## Establish a pre-coating hold point

Before liquid material is mixed, the crew should confirm completed preparation and repairs, clean edges, acceptable slab condition, correct products, and the environmental conditions required by their data sheets. Photographs create a useful baseline. Any departure from the written scope should be approved before it disappears beneath the floor system.

## Photograph the condition that will disappear

Prepared concrete and completed repairs are soon hidden. Request overview photographs after belongings are removed, close-ups after profiling, and a final set before primer or body coat. The record should show cracks, joints, drain edges, doorway band, repaired areas, and any accepted exception.

Photographs do not prove every technical requirement, but they make scope changes and later troubleshooting easier to understand. Pair them with the named product documents and recorded site conditions rather than using appearance as the only acceptance test.

Ask the final proposal to identify preparation method, special-treatment areas, repair allowances or exclusions, edges and drains, dust controls, moisture plan, and the person who accepts the substrate.
