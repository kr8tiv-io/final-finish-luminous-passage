A retail floor has two jobs at once: it supports daily work and forms part of what customers see. The planning challenge is to protect operations, public routes, merchandise, and indoor air while producing a finish that makes sense under real lighting. A colour chip and a promised reopening date are not enough to define that work.

For local scope options, see our [commercial epoxy flooring](/services/commercial-epoxy-flooring).

Begin with the store calendar. Identify delivery days, inventory counts, seasonal resets, tenant restrictions, building access, and quiet customer periods. Then walk the sales floor, stockroom, receiving route, checkout, fitting rooms, entrances, and staff areas. A good scope reflects those differences rather than treating the tenancy as one empty rectangle.

## Follow traffic before choosing the finish

Watch how people and goods move. Mark the entrance path, queue lines, checkout turns, cart or stroller traffic, display changes, pallet-jack routes, and places where employees stand. Note where wet footwear and de-icer arrive during Edmonton winters, where grit collects, and where direct sun or display lighting changes colour and gloss.

Record cracks, joints, hollow patches, old adhesive, previous coatings, moisture signs, damaged thresholds, and floor-height changes. Shelving bases and fixed counters can conceal conditions visible only after clearing. The proposal should state how those discoveries will be documented and authorized.

## Choose appearance with maintenance in the room

Judge a sample on site, not only on a phone or under office light. Place it near the entrance, under bright merchandise lighting, beside wall colours, and in a traffic lane. Consider how colour shows dust, fibres, salt residue, cart marks, and repairs. Gloss can influence reflections and surface variation, but no finish should carry an unsupported promise that it will hide wear.

Ask the cleaning team about chemicals, pads, mops, autoscrubbers, and frequency. Compare that routine with current manufacturer maintenance guidance. Texture, cleanability, and traction belong in one decision. The [solid-colour commercial flooring guide](/resources/solid-colour-commercial-flooring/) has focused sample and repair questions; the [slip-resistance guide](/resources/slip-resistance-floor-coatings/) covers wet zones and operational controls.

## Public access changes the phasing plan

If customers or workers remain nearby, define the work zone as more than coloured tape. Barriers, signage, access control, emergency routes, dust containment, material storage, ventilation, and daily handoff need named responsibility. The store and contractor should agree on who inspects the barrier and who can reopen an area.

The Government of Alberta’s official [OHS Act, Regulation and Code page](https://www.alberta.ca/ohs-act-regulation-code) links to current workplace legislation and search resources. Use it to confirm duties relevant to the people and work involved. A flooring specification does not override the employer’s hazard assessment or the building’s emergency and accessibility planning.

Phasing may preserve trading area, but it creates interfaces. Customers must not be routed across preparation dust, uncured material, tools, hoses, abrupt unfinished edges, or restricted paths. Successive phases need intentional termination lines so they can be prepared and connected according to the selected system.

## Treat indoor-air planning as a system

Collect current safety and product data sheets before scheduling. Include repair products, primers, body coats, topcoats, cleaners, and joint materials—not just the product named in the estimate. Review air intake and exhaust locations, shared corridors, adjacent tenants, security requirements, and whether temporary ventilation could move air toward occupied areas.

The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety provides an official [indoor air quality publication](https://www.ccohs.ca/products/publications/iaq.html) that frames investigation and control as a coordinated process involving building systems, contaminants, occupants, and communication. It is a useful planning reference, while actual controls must follow applicable SDSs, manufacturer instructions, and the site-specific hazard assessment.

Odour alone is not a reliable acceptance test. Agree on written occupancy conditions and who verifies that barriers, ventilation changes, and housekeeping are complete.

## Make the restart sequence explicit

Work backwards from opening time:

1. Empty or protect merchandise and movable fixtures.
2. Isolate the zone and establish approved access and ventilation controls.
3. Remove specified finishes; prepare and inspect concrete.
4. Complete repairs and satisfy their cure or recoat requirements.
5. Apply layers within documented environmental and recoat limits.
6. Inspect colour, texture, terminations, and defects before fixtures return.
7. Reinstall displays without dragging or concentrating loads prematurely.
8. Clean using a permitted method, then complete the reopening handoff.

“Ready tomorrow” is not a schedule. The contractor should state the conditions and product restrictions behind pedestrian access, fixture placement, rolling loads, cleaning, and normal service. Temperatures at Edmonton loading doors may differ from the centre of a heated store, so environmental records should reflect where work occurs.

## What a comparable proposal includes

Ask bidders to describe measured areas, exclusions, substrate findings, preparation, dust control, repairs, joints, layer sequence, colour and texture, sample approval, transitions, ventilation, protection, inspection points, maintenance documents, and staged return to use. Request a process for hidden adhesive, damaged concrete, or unknown old coatings rather than an undefined contingency.

Clarify who moves fixtures, disconnects equipment, removes waste, controls building access, and repairs damage outside the flooring scope. These ordinary details are where a retail project can lose time.
