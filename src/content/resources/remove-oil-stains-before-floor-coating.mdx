Oil on a garage floor is more than a dark mark. It can be contamination at the surface, liquid held in pores, or evidence of an active vehicle or equipment leak. A coating decision should begin by finding out which condition you have. Covering the stain before resolving that question can hide useful evidence and makes it harder to write a defensible preparation scope.

For an Edmonton garage, start with a simple history. Mark where vehicles normally park, where snow melt collects, which stains are recent, what cleaners were used, and whether the area still feels greasy after drying. Photograph the floor in even light. A few notes now give the assessor more than a freshly washed slab with no context.

## Stop the source before treating the concrete

Fix or contain the leak first. Otherwise, freshly prepared concrete can be contaminated again while the project is being planned. Move leaking equipment to a tray, address the vehicle problem, and keep oily rags or absorbent materials off the slab. If an unknown liquid may be hazardous, follow the product label and local disposal requirements rather than treating it as ordinary wash water.

Do not mix cleaners or improvise with solvents. The relevant cleaner, dwell time, agitation, rinsing, recovery, and disposal method depend on the contaminant and the coating manufacturer’s written instructions. A contractor should be able to describe the process without promising that every old stain will disappear visually.

## Cleaning and mechanical preparation do different jobs

Cleaning is intended to remove oil, grease, residue, and cleaning products. Mechanical preparation changes the concrete surface so the specified system can bond to it. One is not a substitute for the other. Grinding or blasting an oily slab can spread contamination, load tooling, or drive residue into adjacent areas; coating over a detergent film creates another problem. The sequence matters.

The [ACI concrete repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) is a primary industry reference that covers condition assessment, surface preparation, repair materials, protective systems, and quality control. Its scope supports treating contamination as part of an evaluated concrete-repair process, not as a cosmetic cleaning step. The project specification and current product data sheets still control the actual method.

An assessment may divide the floor into zones:

- sound, apparently clean concrete;
- visible staining that no longer transfers residue;
- areas that still release oil after cleaning;
- cracked, scaled, or previously patched concrete;
- an old coating with unknown adhesion or contamination beneath it.

That map helps prevent a single preparation method from being applied blindly across the whole garage.

## What can show that the floor is ready?

There is no universal household test that proves oily concrete is coatable. Ask what acceptance check the installer and coating manufacturer require. Depending on the written system, that may include visual inspection, checking for residue after cleaning, a small trial area, surface-profile verification, or an adhesion-related check. The important point is to agree on the evidence before the coating arrives, including who decides whether a questionable zone proceeds, receives more preparation, or is excluded.

Moisture is a separate question. Darkening caused by oil does not establish the slab’s moisture condition, and a dry-looking surface is not a moisture test. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) describes in-situ relative-humidity testing in concrete floor slabs. When the selected manufacturer requires slab-moisture information, the proposal should name the required test method, timing, locations, and acceptance criteria rather than using oil cleanup as a proxy.

If the garage also has scaling, spalls, or cracks around the contaminated area, review the [guide to concrete cracks and spalling before coating](/resources/concrete-cracks-spalling-floor-coating/) before comparing repair allowances. Repairs need compatible preparation and cure conditions of their own.

## Questions that make a quotation clearer

Invite the estimator to be specific. Useful questions include:

1. Which areas are considered contaminated, and how will they be isolated from clean concrete?
2. Which cleaner or treatment is proposed, and does its manufacturer permit the intended use?
3. How will spent cleaner, oily water, and debris be contained and disposed of?
4. What mechanical preparation follows cleaning?
5. What result must be observed before primer or coating is applied?
6. What happens if oil returns during preparation?
7. Are deeply affected areas repairable within the quoted scope, or does work pause for a revised decision?
8. Which current product data sheet governs surface condition, moisture, mixing, and application?

A useful proposal distinguishes included work from contingencies. “Degrease as required” leaves the most important decision undefined. A better scope identifies the observed zones, planned sequence, acceptance check, and process for concealed conditions.

## What homeowners can do before the visit

Avoid applying sealers, paint, acid, or a new degreaser immediately before the assessment. Keep the floor accessible, but leave enough evidence for the contractor to see the stain pattern. Gather the names of products previously used and, if available, the old coating or patch records. Note whether water beads, darkens the concrete unevenly, or carries an oily sheen after cleaning; report those observations without treating them as pass/fail tests.

Edmonton winters add tracked de-icer, sand, water, and road grime to the mix. These are not all “oil,” and they may require different cleaning or repair decisions. The companion guide to [winter salt and concrete-floor damage](/resources/winter-salt-concrete-floor-damage/) helps separate seasonal deposits from scaling or other concrete deterioration.

The practical goal is not a perfectly colourless slab. It is a documented surface condition that the specified coating system permits. Until the contamination, concrete condition, moisture requirements, and acceptance checks are known, the responsible answer is to keep the scope open—and keep this draft in review rather than turning a complex substrate issue into a blanket performance promise.
