## Separate appearance from performance

## Compare the same job, not just two totals

Floor-coating proposals often use different names and levels of detail. One may include repairs and edge work; another may list a system name and price. Build a comparison sheet that forces each bidder to answer the same questions before deciding that one costs more or promises more.

For local scope options, see our [commercial epoxy flooring](/services/commercial-epoxy-flooring).

Begin with identical project facts: measured area, rooms and vertical surfaces, current use, fixed equipment, access hours, known damage, drainage, expected traffic, desired appearance, and the date of the site visit. Attach the same photographs or drawing to each request.

## 1. What did the bidder observe?

Look for notes about cracks, spalls, contamination, previous coatings, moisture indicators, joints, edges, drains, and water entry. Ask which areas were inaccessible. A confident proposal can still acknowledge unknowns.

The [ACI concrete-repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) connects condition assessment with repair, preparation, protective-system selection, and quality control. Use that sequence as the backbone of comparison.

## 2. How will the concrete be prepared?

Record the proposed cleaning and mechanical method, intended surface condition, dust controls, edge tools, old-coating removal, and acceptance check. “Diamond grind” does not explain corners, drains, steps, oil, or weak concrete. Ask what current manufacturer document establishes the required profile.

## 3. Which repairs are included?

List each known crack, joint, spall, low spot, and patch. Identify repair product, preparation, layer sequence, visual expectation, and whether movement may reappear. Note allowances, unit rates, exclusions, and the approval process if preparation reveals more work. Do not assume “crack repair included” means every line receives the same treatment.

## 4. What is the complete system?

Create one row per layer: repair, primer, body coat, broadcast or aggregate, grout coat if any, and topcoat. Request manufacturer, exact product, colour, texture, intended application quantity or thickness as specified, and current technical data sheet. Ask who confirms compatibility if brands are mixed.

A category label such as epoxy, polyurea, or polyaspartic does not describe a complete floor. The [hybrid-system comparison guide](/resources/hybrid-flooring-systems-neutral-guide/) helps decode multi-material proposals.

## 5. How are moisture and conditions handled?

If testing is proposed, compare method, locations, timing, reporting, and product acceptance limits. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) defines an in-situ relative-humidity method; it does not create one pass/fail limit for every product. Also compare how ambient temperature, slab temperature, humidity, and other manufacturer conditions are recorded.

## 6. What happens around the work?

Normalize furniture moving, storage, power, ventilation, barriers, noise, dust, waste, security, washroom or exit access, and protection of adjacent finishes. In an Edmonton garage, ask how overhead-door access and weather are managed. In an occupied business, require a phased access plan and named contacts.

## 7. When can each use resume?

Do not compare a single “done by” time. Ask separately about inspection, foot traffic, furniture, mats, cleaning, vehicles, rolling loads, and chemical exposure. Product documents and actual site conditions should control. Identify who communicates a delay if conditions change.

## 8. What does handover include?

Compare care instructions, system record, photographs, product documents, touch-up expectations, warranty, claim process, and post-installation contact. Read warranty scope and exclusions, not only duration. The [warranty question guide](/resources/floor-coating-warranty-questions/) provides a line-by-line review.

## Turn ambiguity into written assumptions

Use three columns beside every item: **included**, **excluded**, and **unknown pending investigation**. Then add a fourth: **who owns the decision**. This exposes scope gaps without pretending every unknown can be priced before preparation.

Ask how changes are quoted and approved, whether work pauses, and what documentation supports the change. A low initial total can grow if predictable edges or repairs were omitted; a higher total may include work you do not need. The sheet makes that distinction visible.

Before selection, verify legal business name, insurance or safety information relevant to the project, references, and any credentials directly rather than relying on a logo. Do not infer superior quality from a long warranty, branded system name, or fast schedule alone.

## A simple scoring method that avoids false precision

Do not assign a single “quality score” to technical proposals. Instead, mark every row **clear**, **needs clarification**, or **not included**. Resolve the high-consequence rows first: substrate unknowns, moisture, preparation, repairs, compatibility, site safety, and return to use. Appearance options can wait until the system is credible.

Then write a one-sentence consequence beside each exclusion. For example, “Owner moves freezer before start,” “Price changes if old coating contains inaccessible layers,” or “Repair colour may remain visible.” This converts contractual language into operational understanding.

If two proposals still differ, hold a short clarification call using the same unanswered rows for both bidders. Issue any answer that changes scope as a written addendum. Do not let a persuasive meeting replace the document you will later rely on.

## Know when specialist input is needed

A flooring proposal should not silently absorb structural movement, persistent water entry, hazardous-material concerns, electrical relocation, or building ventilation design. When these arise, identify the appropriate qualified professional and define how their finding enters the flooring decision. A contractor who recognizes a boundary is not necessarily avoiding responsibility; they may be keeping the scope accurate.

Store the completed comparison with photographs and clarifications; it explains why the selected scope differed from alternatives.
