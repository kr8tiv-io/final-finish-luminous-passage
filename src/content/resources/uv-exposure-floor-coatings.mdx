Sunlight should be discussed before colour approval, especially where a garage door stays open, a storefront has large glazing, or a bright window casts a defined path across the floor. The issue is often visual consistency: a light-exposed zone may age differently from a shaded zone, and the boundary can become noticeable when furniture or mats move.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

UV behaviour is product-specific. “Epoxy,” “polyaspartic,” “urethane,” or “clear coat” is not enough information to predict a finish.

## Make a light map

Photograph the room in morning, midday, and late light if practical. Mark windows, skylights, overhead doors, south- and west-facing exposure, translucent panels, and any areas shaded by racking or equipment. In Edmonton, seasonal sun angles and long summer daylight can change which portion of a garage or commercial floor receives direct light.

Include artificial sources only where the manufacturer identifies them as relevant. Do not label all indoor lighting “UV” without technical information. The most useful record is the actual product, actual light path, and owner’s sensitivity to colour change.

## Separate colour change from coating failure

A colour or ambering change can be an appearance concern without proving loss of adhesion. Peeling, blistering, cracking, softening, or wear require their own diagnosis. Conversely, a bonded floor can still be unacceptable to an owner if a visible colour boundary conflicts with the design brief.

Write the aesthetic requirement honestly. Is modest variation acceptable in a working garage? Must a retail area remain visually consistent beside windows? Will rugs, pallets, vehicles, or fixtures create shaded outlines? The answer influences system and colour selection, but it does not justify an unsupported permanence claim.

## Ask for exact manufacturer evidence

Request current product data for every visible layer, including the topcoat. Ask what exposure the manufacturer addresses, how colour and clear finishes differ, and what limitations apply. Avoid translating a test into “UV proof” unless the manufacturer uses that exact supported language for the complete application and conditions.

The [topcoat guide](/resources/topcoat-floor-coating-system/) helps place the visible layer inside the full sequence. A finish described as UV stable may still need careful expectation setting for colour, gloss, substrate, repairs, or adjacent materials.

## Approve samples in the real space

View a representative system sample horizontally in direct and indirect light. Include the proposed colour, decorative media, texture, and clear or coloured topcoat. Compare it beside walls, thresholds, drains, and existing floors. Keep the approved sample and product identifiers with the project record.

A short sample review cannot simulate years of exposure. Its purpose is to identify immediate colour, reflection, texture, and lighting concerns. Ask whether darker or lighter options make dust, salt residue, tire marks, or future patches more visible. The [solid-colour commercial flooring guide](/resources/solid-colour-commercial-flooring/) provides a broader mock-up checklist.

## The substrate still controls the foundation

Light exposure does not change the need for condition assessment and preparation. The [ACI PRC-546 concrete repair guide](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623) covers evaluation, repair, preparation, protective systems, and quality control. That framework supports treating the finish as part of a system on sound, properly evaluated concrete.

Do not add a different topcoat solely from an online UV claim. The new layer must be compatible with the underlying coats and applied within manufacturer requirements. A late substitution can change adhesion, texture, gloss, cure, and maintenance as well as light response.

## Moisture is unrelated evidence

Sunlight through a door can warm the surface and make it look dry, but that is not a slab-moisture evaluation. [ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) describes in-situ relative-humidity testing in concrete slabs. If required by the specified coating, identify test method, timing, locations, and product-specific criteria. UV planning and moisture testing answer different questions.

## Design boundaries deliberately

If differently exposed zones are unavoidable, place transitions where they make visual sense rather than at an accidental roller edge. A colour break, line marking, room boundary, or full-zone application may make future variation less distracting. Any transition still needs compatible detailing.

For an existing floor with a sun-faded path, do not assume a local clear coat will restore colour. Clean a discreet area only as permitted, identify the old system, and ask whether repair or recoat is technically suitable. New and aged material may not match.

## Put these points in the quotation

- mapped direct-light and shaded zones;
- exact visible products and colours;
- source of manufacturer UV or colour-retention information;
- representative sample and approval process;
- acceptable appearance variation and excluded promises;
- preparation and complete compatible layer sequence;
- phase lines, thresholds, windows, doors, and future repair expectations;
- maintenance products and restrictions;
- staged cure and return-to-service conditions.

No responsible article can promise a specific colour-retention period for an unnamed system in an unmeasured room. ## Preserve a baseline after installation

Take dated photographs from the same positions in consistent light after handover. Include direct-light boundaries, shaded areas, thresholds, and the approved sample. Keep cleaners, mats, and storage patterns in the maintenance record. If colour later appears different, first rule out residue, gloss change, or movable shading using only manufacturer-permitted cleaning. Baseline images will not prove cause, but they give the manufacturer or assessor far better evidence than memory. Inspect adhesion, wear, and cracks separately from appearance, and do not apply a local clear coat until the existing layers and compatible repair procedure are confirmed.
