Furniture can go back only when the written restrictions for the exact floor system allow that particular load and movement. “Dry to touch”, foot traffic, light placement, heavy point loads, casters, rugs, cleaning, and normal service can be different stages. There is no responsible universal number.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

Ask for the move-back plan before the room is emptied. That lets you arrange storage, movers, building access, and contingency time without pressuring a new finish.

## Inventory loads, not just furniture names

List each item and how it contacts the floor. A sofa on broad feet differs from a loaded shelving upright, piano caster, safe, filing cabinet, wheeled chair, retail display, or workbench. Note whether it must be rolled, slid, assembled in place, anchored, or levelled. Include rugs, rubber-backed mats, plastic bins, plants, appliances, and anything that could trap moisture or transfer material.

Send that list to the installer or manufacturer. Ask for written permission or restrictions for each category. If load information is needed, obtain it from the equipment or furniture documentation rather than guessing.

## Build a staged handover

A clear sequence can distinguish:

1. contractor inspection and protection removal;
2. controlled pedestrian access;
3. placement of light, clean items;
4. heavy or concentrated loads;
5. casters and rolling traffic;
6. rugs, mats, and floor protectors;
7. wet cleaning and normal room use.

The stages and conditions come from current documents for the installed primer, coats, repair materials, and topcoat. Temperature, humidity, substrate, ventilation, layer thickness, and recoat timing can affect the applicable schedule. Do not borrow a time from a different product or project.

The [floor-coating cure guide](/resources/how-epoxy-floor-coatings-cure/) explains why cure is a process rather than a single moment. Keep the contractor’s dated handover notice with the product record.

## Prepare the move before the green light

Clean furniture feet, wheels, and protectors away from the new floor. Embedded grit can scratch during placement. Verify that pads, glides, mats, tapes, and protective boards are compatible with both furniture and coating; do not assume rubber, plastic, adhesive, cardboard, or plywood is harmless.

Plan enough people and lifting equipment to place items without dragging. Use routes and protection approved by the installer. Do not pivot a heavy cabinet on one corner or roll a concentrated load across a floor permitted only for foot traffic. Keep doors and Edmonton winter grit controlled during the move.

If anchors are required, coordinate drilling, dust, edge distances, and any effect on the floor system with qualified parties. A coating installer cannot approve structural anchorage by implication.

## Ventilation continues according to the plan

Occupancy and cure are related but not identical decisions. Health Canada’s official page on [volatile organic compounds](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/indoor-air-contaminants/volatile-organic-compounds.html) explains that VOCs can come from paints, coatings, and other household products and discusses exposure reduction. Use product safety data, the contractor’s controls, and current health guidance rather than judging readiness by odour alone.

Health Canada also provides practical [indoor-air-quality guidance for homes](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/improve-indoor-air-quality-in-your-home.html), including source control and ventilation considerations around renovation products. Follow the site-specific ventilation plan and do not disable exhaust early merely because furniture delivery has arrived. In workplaces, applicable OHS responsibilities also need review.

## Mats and rugs deserve a separate question

A mat can reduce tracked soil, but an unapproved backing may mark a finish, trap moisture, or interfere with cure. Ask when the manufacturer permits coverings and which materials are compatible. The same applies to taped protection and temporary floor coverings.

At Edmonton entries, keep exterior moisture, sand, and de-icer from being sealed under mats. Establish a cleaning and drying routine after the floor is in service.

## What if the floor is marked during move-back?

Stop and photograph the area. Record the item, route, load, protector, and time. Do not scrub, solvent-wipe, sand, or add a touch-up without written direction. A transfer mark, indentation, scuff, and cut through a layer need different responses. The [scratch-maintenance guide](/resources/scratch-maintenance-coated-floor/) starts with identification and least-aggressive permitted cleaning.

Keep people off soft, lifted, or damaged areas until the installer assesses them. Moving the rest of the room around a fresh repair may create another scheduling stage.

## Questions to settle in writing

- Which product documents set each return-to-use stage?
- Who issues the release, and how are site conditions considered?
- Which items, wheels, mats, pads, and protection were disclosed?
- Who moves heavy items and controls the route?
- When are cleaning, water, and normal occupancy permitted?
- What ventilation continues after application?
- What is the process if the schedule or conditions change?

A written staged plan is safer for the floor and far easier on move day than a last-minute guess.

## Give movers a one-page instruction

Translate the installer’s release into a simple sheet naming the permitted route, protection, lifting method, prohibited dragging, approved items, and person who can answer questions. Photograph the clear floor before delivery and the room after placement. Keep food, plants, pets, customers, and uninvolved workers outside the controlled move where appropriate. If the crew encounters a tacky area, unexpected odour, soft finish, loose edge, or damaged protection, stop that route and contact the responsible contractor. Delivery pressure should never override written restrictions.
