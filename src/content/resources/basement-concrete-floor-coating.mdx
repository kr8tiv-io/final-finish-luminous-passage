A basement coating should be the last decision in a short chain, not the first. Begin by deciding whether the room has a water problem, a concrete problem, or simply an unfinished surface. A coating can change colour, cleanability, texture, and light reflection. It cannot redirect groundwater, repair a failed drain, or make an unsound slab sound.

For local scope options, see our [residential epoxy flooring](/services/residential-epoxy-flooring).

## 1. Rule out active water entry

Inspect after snowmelt or heavy rain if possible. Record where dampness appears: at a wall-floor joint, around a penetration, beneath stored objects, or across the open slab. Photograph mineral deposits, dark areas, peeling paint, and previous patches. Note sump operation, plumbing leaks, downspout issues, and whether exterior drainage work is underway.

This distinction matters because a moisture reading does not identify the source of liquid water. ASTM F2170 measures relative humidity inside a concrete slab at the time of testing; ASTM explicitly notes that results describe the slab’s moisture condition only when the test is performed ([ASTM F2170-19a](https://store.astm.org/f2170-19a.html)). Active leakage therefore calls for building or drainage diagnosis before a flooring specification.

**Stop the coating conversation temporarily** if water is visibly entering, puddles recur without an indoor spill, or the proposed fix is described only as “sealing it in.” Resolve the source, allow the assembly to stabilize, and then reassess.

## 2. Map the slab rather than judging one clean patch

Empty enough of the room to see the perimeter and the areas beneath rugs, cabinets, or old flooring. Mark the following on a simple sketch:

- cracks, including whether their edges are level or offset;
- hollow, weak, flaking, or spalled concrete;
- old adhesive, paint, curing compound, or unknown sealer;
- floor drains, control joints, columns, and penetrations;
- low areas that hold wash water;
- repairs that differ in colour or texture from the original slab.

ACI’s current concrete-repair guide treats condition evaluation, removal of unsound concrete, material selection, surface preparation, and quality control as connected parts of a repair project ([ACI PRC-546-23](https://www.concrete.org/store/productdetail.aspx?ItemID=54623)). That is a useful mindset for a basement: do not select a finish until the repair scope is visible.

A crack is not automatically a coating disqualifier. What matters is why it exists, whether it moves, whether water passes through it, and how the proposed repair will interact with the finished surface. An offset crack, recurring wet crack, or widespread deterioration deserves evaluation beyond a cosmetic fill.

## 3. Ask for a defined moisture test and an acceptance rule

“We checked for moisture” is incomplete. A useful proposal states:

1. the test method and edition;
2. the number and placement of test locations;
3. when the readings will be taken;
4. the product manufacturer’s acceptable limits;
5. what changes if a result exceeds those limits.

In-situ relative-humidity testing under ASTM F2170 is quantitative and uses probes placed in the slab. It is different from a handheld surface reading or an informal plastic-sheet observation ([ASTM F2170-19a](https://store.astm.org/f2170-19a.html)). The number alone is not a universal pass/fail score: compare the documented result with the written requirements for the complete proposed system.

For an older slab, ask whether a vapour retarder is known to exist beneath it. “Unknown” is a legitimate answer and should remain a documented uncertainty, not be converted into a guess.

## 4. Define what the room must tolerate

The right finish for a mechanical room may be wrong for a bedroom or workshop. Write down actual use before discussing chemistry.

| Basement use | Conditions to disclose | Decisions to settle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laundry or utility | Drips, drain access, appliance movement | Edge details, repairs, cleaning method |
| Home gym | Rubber equipment, point loads, sweat, mats | Texture, mat compatibility, maintenance |
| Workshop | Oils, rolling tools, dropped objects | Chemical exposure, impact expectations, repairability |
| Family or finished room | Occupancy, furniture, adjacent HVAC returns | Odour plan, appearance sample, return-to-use criteria |
| Storage | Shelving anchors, cardboard, limited airflow | Moisture history, access, practical cleanability |

Avoid promises such as “waterproof,” “maintenance-free,” or “permanent” unless the proposal defines exactly what is warranted, on which substrate, and under which exclusions.

## 5. Plan ventilation as part of the scope

Product selection, isolation, and ventilation belong in the work plan when the basement connects to occupied rooms. Health Canada recommends reducing pollutant sources and increasing ventilation during renovation activities, while following product labels and safety information ([Health Canada indoor-air guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/improve-indoor-air-quality-in-your-home.html)).

Ask where air will enter, where it will exhaust, and whether the route could send vapour or dust into another window, doorway, or air intake. Also identify occupants who may require a more conservative plan. Odour is not a reliable exposure meter; the current safety data sheet and the site-specific controls are the proper starting points.

## 6. Make the quote auditable

Before approving work, look for a written sequence rather than a coating name alone:

- protect adjacent rooms and control preparation dust;
- identify the mechanical preparation method and target surface condition;
- remove or repair unsound concrete;
- record moisture-test results and the manufacturer’s acceptance threshold;
- name every primer, repair material, body coat, broadcast material, and topcoat;
- state how joints, edges, drains, steps, and penetrations will be treated;
- define inspection points before layers conceal the substrate;
- provide product data and safety data sheets;
- state conditions for foot traffic, furniture, and normal use.

The best next step is a site assessment with the room exposed and its moisture history written down. Request options if the findings are uncertain: repair and proceed, test again after corrective work, select a compatible system, or postpone coating. That makes “not ready yet” an acceptable project outcome rather than a surprise found during installation.

*This guide is general planning information. Water entry, structural movement, hazardous materials, and product-specific safety or compatibility questions require qualified assessment and the current manufacturer instructions.*
