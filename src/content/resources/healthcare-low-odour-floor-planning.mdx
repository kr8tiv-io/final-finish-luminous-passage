## Start with the site

## Begin with people and operations

“Low odour” sounds reassuring, but it is not a complete exposure assessment or a healthcare approval. A care-space flooring plan should begin with occupants, clinical operations, air movement, infection-prevention procedures, and emergency access. Only then should the team compare products.

For local scope options, see our [commercial epoxy flooring](/services/commercial-epoxy-flooring).

Identify who could be affected: patients or residents, visitors, staff, contractors, and occupants in connected areas. Note sensitivities known to the facility team without collecting unnecessary personal health information. Map services that cannot stop, including medication routes, washrooms, lifts, nurse-call access, egress, and cleaning stations.

## Put the right people around the table

Before scheduling, assign one decision-maker from facilities and involve infection prevention and control, occupational health and safety, housekeeping, clinical operations, and ventilation specialists as the facility requires. The coating contractor should not be asked to invent the facility's infection-control or occupant-relocation policy.

Use a short written brief covering work zones, isolation boundaries, access routes, noise periods, shutdown approvals, air-handling decisions, cleaning validation, and communication. The [commercial flooring downtime guide](/resources/commercial-flooring-downtime-planning/) can help turn operational needs into a phased schedule.

## Odour, VOCs, and product documentation

Odour intensity does not quantify health risk, and an absence of noticeable smell does not establish that a space is ready for occupancy. Health Canada explains that [volatile organic compounds can come from paints, varnishes, glues, and other products](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/indoor-air-contaminants/volatile-organic-compounds.html), and advises following label directions, using proper ventilation, and choosing lower-emission products where possible.

For every primer, repair material, body coat, topcoat, cleaner, and solvent, request the current technical data sheet and safety data sheet. Have the facility's qualified reviewer assess hazards, protective measures, ventilation requirements, storage, spill response, and application restrictions. A marketing phrase such as “zero VOC” or “low odour” should never replace the complete documents or the facility's own acceptance criteria.

## Trace how air and dust can travel

Floor preparation may create dust and noise before any liquid product is opened. Walk the area with facilities staff and identify supply and return grilles, pressure relationships, doors, penetrations, lifts, ceiling spaces, and shared corridors. Decide who may alter air handling and how the decision will be documented. Do not ask a flooring crew to shut down building systems without facility authorization.

Containment should address the actual preparation method and traffic pattern. Define where workers change or clean footwear, where waste is sealed, and how tools leave the zone. [Health Canada's indoor-air guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/improve-indoor-air-quality-in-your-home.html) recommends increasing ventilation during renovation activities and using products as directed; in a care facility, the facility's technical team must translate that general principle into a building-specific plan.

## Build the sequence around hold points

A practical phase can be described as a controlled sequence:

1. The facility releases the cleared zone and confirms barriers and routes.
2. The contractor documents substrate conditions and preparation controls.
3. The agreed reviewer accepts preparation and any newly discovered repair scope.
4. Products are checked against current documents, lot records, and required conditions.
5. Application proceeds while access and ventilation controls remain active.
6. The floor is protected through its stated recoat and cure stages.
7. Housekeeping performs the approved post-work cleaning.
8. Facilities or clinical operations authorizes reopening against written criteria.

Avoid using “dry to touch” as the reopening rule. Walking, furniture, cleaning, rolling loads, chemical exposure, and full service may have different manufacturer restrictions. Record who owns each decision and what evidence they need.

## Design for cleaning and care delivery

Review seams, edges, drains, coves, transitions, door clearances, fixed equipment, and repair boundaries with housekeeping and infection-control staff. Ask for the coating manufacturer's cleaning and disinfectant-compatibility information relevant to the products the facility actually uses. Compatibility should be documented; a generic claim of “chemical resistance” is not enough.

Traction also needs a use-based discussion. Consider footwear, wet cleaning, mobility aids, carts, and surface texture. More texture may affect cleaning effort, while a smoother surface may behave differently when contaminated. The [slip-resistance guide](/resources/slip-resistance-floor-coatings/) explains why a test value alone cannot replace real-use analysis.

## The handover package

Before reopening, collect approved product documents, colour and system record, preparation and condition photos, application dates, maintenance instructions, restrictions, incident contacts, and any facility sign-offs. Document incomplete punch-list items and protect those areas.

## Rehearse the exceptions

Before work starts, run through three credible disruptions: a phase does not reach its next milestone on schedule, a barrier is breached, or an occupant reports discomfort. For each, state who stops work, who assesses the concern, where occupants go, how the area is secured, and who authorizes resumption. Keep current contact information available outside the contained zone.

Also plan for unexpected substrate damage discovered during preparation. The flooring crew should not continue simply to protect the calendar. Facilities and clinical operations need a documented route to review the repair, revise access, and notify affected teams. A controlled delay is safer operationally than an unrecorded change beneath a finished surface.

Retain the approved phase plan and incident log with facility records so maintenance teams can understand the controls and installed materials.
